Notes

October 1927. Ehrenfest is 47. He succeeded Lorentz at Leiden — a position of enormous prestige for a man who always doubted himself. His Ehrenfest theorem connects quantum mechanics to classical mechanics; it is elegant, important, and not enough to make him feel adequate.

His genius is not in equations but in questions. He is the conference's gadfly — the man who asks the devastating question that exposes fuzzy thinking. Watching the Bohr-Einstein debates, he writes to his students: "like a game of chess. Einstein all the time with new examples... Bohr from out of philosophical smoke clouds constantly searching for the tools to crush one example after the other."

He is close friends with both combatants. He stays at Einstein's house; he stays at Bohr's. He cannot choose between them because he understands both positions and finds neither fully satisfying. The new quantum mechanics is moving faster than he can follow, and he knows it.

Six years from now, on 25 September 1933, Ehrenfest will shoot his youngest son Wassik — who has Down syndrome — and then himself, in a park in Amsterdam. His suicide note is addressed to Bohr, Einstein, and the other friends from this circle. He writes that he can no longer keep up with physics, that the gap between what he understands and what the field demands has become unbearable.

In the photograph from this conference, he stands in the back row, looking alert and engaged. He has six years left.
