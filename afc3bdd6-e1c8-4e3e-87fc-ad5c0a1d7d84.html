Notes

October 1927. Heisenberg is 25 years old. He published matrix mechanics two years ago — a complete mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics that arrived before Schrödinger's wave equation. The uncertainty principle is six months old, published in March 1927.

He is at the height of his powers, part of what the older generation calls the Knabenphysik — the physics of boys. He, Pauli, Dirac, and Jordan are remaking the foundations of physics while still in their twenties. Bohr is his mentor; Copenhagen is his intellectual home.

At Solvay he watches his mentor debate the most famous scientist in the world — and win. The uncertainty principle, his principle, is at the center of the argument. Einstein attacks it; Bohr defends it. Heisenberg stands with Bohr.

He does not yet know what the next eighteen years will bring: the Nobel Prize at 31, the decision to stay in Germany under the Nazis, the uranium project, the ambiguity that will haunt his reputation forever. In October 1927 he is simply brilliant, young, and certain that the universe is uncertain.

He stands in the back row of the photograph, far right, looking like what he is: a young man who has just changed the world and knows it.
