Solvay Conference 1927—11

Event

The Fifth Solvay International Conference on Physics, 24–29 October 1927, Brussels. Theme: "Electrons and Photons."

29 attendees, 17 of whom were or became Nobel laureates. The conference is remembered as the site of the Bohr-Einstein debates over the interpretation of quantum mechanics — debates that defined the philosophical fault lines of 20th-century physics.

Hendrik Lorentz, president of the conference, died five months later. The group photograph taken on the steps of the Institut International de Physique Solvay has become one of the most famous images in the history of science.

Persons

Albert Einstein (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Albert Einstein—7)

Person

October 1927. Einstein is 48. He has held the Nobel Prize for six years (awarded 1921, for the photoelectric effect — not for relativity, which the committee considered too controversial). General relativity is twelve years old and confirmed by Eddington's eclipse observations. He is the most famous scientist alive.

But Einstein has come to Brussels to fight. Quantum mechanics — the theory his own photoelectric work helped launch — has grown into something he cannot accept. Bohr and Heisenberg's Copenhagen interpretation says the universe is fundamentally probabilistic: particles do not have definite properties until measured. Einstein finds this intolerable.

His objection is not mere conservatism. He believes quantum mechanics violates locality — that it requires instantaneous action at a distance, "spooky action at a distance" as he will later call it. At the conference he constructs thought experiment after thought experiment to reveal contradictions in the quantum framework. Bohr answers each one, sometimes staying up all night to find the rebuttal.

Einstein will lose this debate — not because he is wrong about locality (the EPR paradox he will publish in 1935 identifies a real and still-unresolved tension), but because the Copenhagen interpretation works. It predicts experimental results with extraordinary precision. Physics will move on without him.

He sits in the front row of the photograph, arms crossed, looking neither defeated nor persuaded.

Arthur Compton (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Arthur Compton—7)

Person

October 1927. Compton is 35. He is about to share the Nobel Prize with C.T.R. Wilson — the announcement comes just weeks after this conference.

The Compton effect, discovered in 1922-23, is one of the experimental facts driving everything being debated in this room. He showed that X-rays scattering off electrons behave exactly as particle collisions — transferring momentum in discrete quanta. This was direct, inescapable evidence for the particle nature of light, alongside the wave interference patterns that were equally well established.

Wave-particle duality — the central theme of this conference, titled "Electrons and Photons" — exists as a scientific problem partly because of Compton's results. Bohr's complementarity, Heisenberg's uncertainty, de Broglie's matter waves — all are attempts to make sense of a world where Compton's particles and Young's interference fringes are both undeniably real.

He does not yet know that he will later chair the committee that launches the Manhattan Project's plutonium program. In October 1927, he is an experimentalist whose data has forced the theorists to rethink reality.

Auguste Piccard—7

Person

Auguste Piccard (1884–1962). Swiss-Belgian physicist and explorer. Professor at the Free University of Brussels. Best known for his record-breaking stratospheric balloon flights in 1931–1932, reaching altitudes above 15,000 meters in a pressurized gondola of his own design.

Later invented the bathyscaphe for deep-sea exploration; his son Jacques used an improved version to reach the bottom of the Mariana Trench in 1960. At the Solvay Conference he served as a scientific secretary alongside Henriot. His tall, thin figure and wild hair reportedly inspired Herge's Professor Calculus in the Tintin comics.

C.T.R. Wilson (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← C.T.R. Wilson—7)

Person

October 1927. Wilson is 58. He is about to win the Nobel Prize — announced just weeks after this conference, shared with Arthur Compton, who sits nearby in the middle row.

His cloud chamber, invented in 1911, made the invisible visible. For the first time, you could see the tracks of individual particles — alpha particles, electrons, scattered photons. The theoretical debates swirling around this conference are about particles and waves; Wilson built the instrument that shows you the particles are real.

The cloud chamber started from an unlikely place: Wilson was a meteorologist fascinated by the optical phenomena of clouds on Scottish mountains. He built a chamber to study cloud formation and discovered it could detect ionizing radiation. The beautiful photographs of particle tracks it produced became essential evidence in the quantum debates.

He is the experimentalist among theoreticians — quiet, Scottish, methodical. While Bohr and Einstein argue about whether God plays dice, Wilson's cloud chamber shows you the dice landing.

Charles-Eugene Guye—7

Person

Charles-Eugène Guye (1866–1942). Swiss experimental physicist. Professor at the University of Geneva, where he conducted precision experiments on the variation of mass with velocity — among the earliest experimental confirmations of relativistic mechanics.

Einstein was his student at the Zurich Polytechnic (ETH). Guye's experimental work provided important early evidence supporting special relativity. He was one of the lesser-known but respected experimentalists at the 1927 Solvay Conference.

Edouard Herzen—7

Person

Edouard Herzen (1877–1936). Belgian physical chemist. Grandson of the Russian political thinker Alexander Herzen. Served as the scientific liaison to the Solvay family and the Solvay Institutes, playing an essential organizational role in the Solvay Conferences.

While not a major researcher himself, Herzen was crucial to the conferences as an intermediary between the industrialist-philanthropists funding the meetings and the physicists who attended them. His position reflected the Belgian institutional framework that made the Solvay Conferences possible.

Emile Henriot—7

Person

Emile Henriot (1885–1961). French physical chemist. Professor at the University of Paris. Demonstrated the natural radioactivity of potassium and rubidium, showing that radioactivity was not confined to heavy elements.

Pioneer of ultracentrifuge technology, developing air-turbine-driven centrifuges capable of extremely high rotational speeds. At the 1927 Solvay Conference he served as one of the scientific secretaries alongside Piccard. His experimental work, while less famous than that of the quantum theorists present, was valued for its precision.

Erwin Schrödinger (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Erwin Schrodinger—7)

Person

October 1927. Schrödinger is 40. His wave equation is barely a year old — published in 1926 in a burst of four papers that arrived like a thunderclap. He formalized de Broglie's wave hypothesis into a rigorous mathematical framework. The equation works beautifully. The question is what it means.

Schrödinger wants it to mean that particles are actually waves — that the wave function describes a real, physical field. Born says no: the wave function gives you probabilities, not physical waves. Heisenberg's matrix mechanics gives the same answers through a completely different formalism. Dirac has just shown the two are mathematically equivalent.

Schrödinger sides with Einstein and de Broglie against the Copenhagen interpretation. He finds Born's probabilistic reading of his own equation deeply unsatisfying. Years later he will invent the famous cat thought experiment (1935) precisely to expose the absurdity of quantum superposition taken literally.

At the conference he represents one half of the mathematical toolkit — wave mechanics versus Heisenberg's matrix mechanics. The mathematics is settled; the meaning is not. He will share the Nobel with Dirac in 1933, flee Austria in 1938, and spend the rest of his career in Dublin, still arguing that the wave function should be taken literally.

He never comes to terms with Born's interpretation. Neither does Einstein. The universe does not appear to care.

Hendrik Kramers (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Hendrik Kramers—7)

Person

October 1927. Kramers is 32. He has spent nearly a decade as Bohr's chief assistant in Copenhagen — longer than anyone. He co-developed the Kramers-Heisenberg dispersion formula with the young Heisenberg in 1925, a key bridge between the old and new quantum theory.

He is the man closest to Bohr's thinking, the one who has absorbed the Copenhagen spirit most deeply. When Bohr presents complementarity, Kramers understands it better than almost anyone in the room. The "K" in the WKB approximation is his.

But there is a cost to being Bohr's lieutenant for so long. Kramers has his own ideas — his own contributions to quantum mechanics are substantial — yet he is often overshadowed by the more dramatic figures around him. Heisenberg, five years younger, has already surpassed him in reputation.

He will eventually leave Copenhagen, succeed Ehrenfest at Leiden in 1934, and build his own legacy. But in October 1927 he stands with Bohr, the loyal collaborator, watching the framework they helped build transform physics.

Hendrik Lorentz (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Hendrik Lorentz—7)

Person

October 1927. Lorentz is 74. He has chaired every Solvay Conference since the first in 1911. He won the Nobel Prize twenty-five years ago. His Lorentz transformations became the mathematical foundation of Einstein's special relativity.

He will be dead in four months.

This is his last conference, though no one knows it. He presides over the Bohr-Einstein debates with the authority of a man who has watched physics transform from classical certainty to quantum uncertainty across his lifetime. He is respected by everyone in the room — perhaps the only person both Einstein and Bohr would defer to.

When he dies in February 1928, Einstein and Curie attend his funeral. Ehrenfest succeeds him at Leiden. Langevin succeeds him as president of the Solvay conferences. The photograph from this conference becomes, in retrospect, a portrait of the last time the old guard and the new sat together under his chairmanship.

Irving Langmuir—7

Person

Irving Langmuir (1881–1957). American chemist and physicist. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1932 for discoveries in surface chemistry — particularly the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, which describes the relationship between gas pressure and the amount adsorbed on a surface.

Spent most of his career at the General Electric Research Laboratory in Schenectady, New York, where he developed the gas-filled incandescent lamp and made fundamental contributions to plasma physics (he coined the term "plasma"). Also pioneered cloud seeding for weather modification. One of the few industrial scientists to win a Nobel Prize and to attend the Solvay Conferences.

Jules-Emile Verschaffelt—7

Person

Jules-Émile Verschaffelt (1870–1955). Belgian experimental physicist. Worked under Heike Kamerlingh Onnes at the cryogenics laboratory in Leiden before returning to Belgium as professor at the University of Ghent.

Served as the principal organizer and secretary of the Solvay Conferences for many years, handling the logistical and administrative work that made the meetings run. While his own research in experimental thermodynamics and critical phenomena was competent, his lasting contribution was institutional — ensuring the Solvay Conferences brought the right people together at the right time.

Leon Brillouin—7

Person

Léon Brillouin (1889–1969). French-American physicist. Son of the physicist Marcel Brillouin. Known for Brillouin zones in solid-state physics, which describe the fundamental domains of reciprocal lattice space in crystals — essential to the theory of electronic band structure.

Co-developed the WKB (Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin) semiclassical approximation method for solving quantum mechanical problems. Later made significant contributions to information theory, connecting thermodynamic entropy to information (Brillouin's negentropy principle). Emigrated to the United States during World War II and remained there, working at Columbia and IBM.

Louis de Broglie (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Louis de Broglie—7)

Person

October 1927. De Broglie is 35, the 7th Duc de Broglie — a French aristocrat who chose physics over the family tradition of diplomacy and politics. His doctoral thesis (1924) proposed that all matter has wave-like properties. Einstein read it and called it "a first feeble ray of light on this worst of our physics enigmas."

Lorentz specifically invited him to present his pilot-wave theory at this conference. It is a deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics — particles have definite trajectories guided by a "pilot wave." It offers an alternative to the Copenhagen interpretation's embrace of fundamental randomness.

It does not go well. Pauli, 27 years old and already feared for his devastating critiques, attacks the theory. De Broglie cannot answer Pauli's objection. The audience moves on. Einstein supports de Broglie's instinct but not his formalism. Schrödinger's wave equation was developed partly to put de Broglie's intuition on rigorous mathematical ground, but the probabilistic interpretation (Born) has captured it.

De Broglie abandons pilot-wave theory after this conference. He will not return to it for twenty-five years, until David Bohm independently rediscovers and extends the idea in 1952. The Nobel committee awards him the prize in 1929 anyway — for the wave nature of matter itself, not for the interpretation that was rejected here.

This conference is where a viable alternative to Copenhagen was killed. The road not taken.

Marie Curie (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Marie Curie—7)

Person

October 1927. Curie is 59. She holds two Nobel Prizes — Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911) — and remains the only person to have won in two different sciences. Pierre has been dead for twenty-one years.

She is visibly exhausted. Decades of handling radioactive materials without protection have ravaged her health: her fingers are burned, her eyesight is failing, she carries test tubes of radium in her pockets and stores them in her desk. She will not live another seven years.

At the conference she represents the older generation of experimentalists. The theoretical battles between Bohr and Einstein are not her war — her domain is the laboratory, the measurable, the material. But she understands what is at stake. She attended the first Solvay Conference in 1911, the only woman then as now. She has watched the theoretical edifice of classical physics crack and fall across sixteen years of these gatherings.

In the photograph she sits in the front row between Lorentz and Einstein. She looks tired, resolute, and entirely unimpressed by the theoretical fireworks around her.

Martin Knudsen—7

Person

Martin Knudsen (1871–1949). Danish physicist. Professor at the University of Copenhagen, where he was a colleague of Niels Bohr. Known for his pioneering experimental work on molecular gas flow at low pressures.

The Knudsen number — the ratio of molecular mean free path to a characteristic physical length — is named after him and is fundamental to rarefied gas dynamics. He also developed the Knudsen cell, used for measuring vapor pressures. His work bridged experimental physics and physical chemistry.

Max Born (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Max Born—7)

Person

October 1927. Born is 44. He co-presents with Heisenberg on matrix mechanics at Lorentz's request. His probabilistic interpretation of the wave function — the Born rule, published just last year — is one of the pillars being debated.

The Born rule says the wave function gives you probabilities, not certainties. The square of the amplitude tells you the likelihood of finding a particle in a particular state. This is the idea Einstein cannot accept. "God does not play dice," Einstein tells Born directly. They are personal friends. The disagreement will last the rest of their lives.

Born supervised both Heisenberg and Pauli at Göttingen. The matrix mechanics that Heisenberg published in 1925 grew directly from Born's research program. Born is the senior figure, the institutional anchor — but it is his students who get the glory. Heisenberg will win the Nobel in 1932 alone, without Born. The Nobel committee will not recognize the Born rule until 1954 — twenty-eight years after he proposed it, three years after Einstein's death.

In October 1927 he is at the peak of his contributions. The recognition will take decades to catch up.

Max Planck (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Max Planck—7)

Person

October 1927. Planck is 69 years old — the oldest active physicist at the conference. He won the Nobel nine years ago. He retired from the University of Berlin last year.

He is the man who started all of this. In December 1900, to explain black-body radiation, he introduced the quantum of action — energy comes in discrete packets. He called it "an act of desperation." He spent the next decade trying to reconcile his own discovery with classical physics. He failed.

Now, twenty-seven years later, he sits in the front row and watches the young men take his quantum to places he never intended. Born's probabilistic interpretation, Heisenberg's uncertainty, Bohr's complementarity — these are not refinements of his idea, they are a revolution built on his foundation. Like Einstein, he is uncomfortable. Unlike Einstein, he does not fight. He listens.

He has nine more years of Nobel Prize in him but the worst is still ahead: his son Erwin will be executed by the Nazis in 1945 for his role in the plot to assassinate Hitler. In October 1927, none of that has happened yet. He is simply the elder statesman of physics, watching his revolution outgrow him.

Niels Bohr (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Niels Bohr—7)

Person

October 1927. Bohr is 42. He received the Nobel Prize five years ago. His Institute of Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen has become the world center for the new quantum theory — the young physicists call it "the Copenhagen spirit." Heisenberg, Pauli, and Dirac have all passed through his institute.

Bohr arrives at Solvay with the Copenhagen interpretation fully formed. His complementarity principle, presented for the first time at Como just weeks before, argues that quantum objects have complementary properties (wave and particle) that cannot be observed simultaneously — and that this is not a limitation of measurement but a fundamental feature of nature.

The conference becomes his arena. Einstein attacks the quantum framework with increasingly ingenious thought experiments at breakfast; Bohr spends the day demolishing each one. Ehrenfest, watching from the sidelines, writes to his students: "like a game of chess. Einstein all the time with new examples... Bohr from out of philosophical smoke clouds constantly searching for the tools to crush one example after the other."

Bohr wins. Not by proving Einstein wrong — Einstein's concerns about locality will prove prescient — but by demonstrating that the Copenhagen interpretation is internally consistent and experimentally productive. The physics community follows Bohr. The old certainty is gone.

Owen Richardson—7

Person

Owen Richardson (1879–1959). British physicist. Nobel Prize in Physics 1928 for his work on thermionic emission — the discharge of electrons from heated surfaces. His research established the Richardson equation (sometimes called the Richardson-Dushman equation), which describes the relationship between emission current and temperature.

Professor at King's College London and later at Princeton. His work on thermionic emission laid the foundation for vacuum tube electronics, which dominated electrical engineering until the transistor era.

Paul Dirac (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Paul Dirac—7)

Person

October 1927. Dirac is 25, the same age as Heisenberg. He completed his PhD at Cambridge just one year ago. His transformation theory, published earlier this year, provided the first unified mathematical framework connecting Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave mechanics — showing they were the same theory in different mathematical clothing.

He says almost nothing at the conference. This is characteristic. While Einstein and Bohr debate philosophy, Dirac works on equations. Heisenberg will later recall that during a dinner discussion about religion and physics, Bohr made subtle arguments, Einstein pushed back — and Dirac said flatly that he did not see the need for the hypothesis of God.

Within a year he will publish the Dirac equation, unifying quantum mechanics with special relativity and predicting the existence of antimatter — a prediction confirmed four years later. He will share the Nobel Prize with Schrödinger in 1933, at age 31.

In the photograph he stands in the middle row, looking slightly elsewhere, as if the social occasion around him is a minor inconvenience interrupting something more important happening inside his head.

Paul Ehrenfest (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Paul Ehrenfest—7)

Person

October 1927. Ehrenfest is 47. He succeeded Lorentz at Leiden — a position of enormous prestige for a man who always doubted himself. His Ehrenfest theorem connects quantum mechanics to classical mechanics; it is elegant, important, and not enough to make him feel adequate.

His genius is not in equations but in questions. He is the conference's gadfly — the man who asks the devastating question that exposes fuzzy thinking. Watching the Bohr-Einstein debates, he writes to his students: "like a game of chess. Einstein all the time with new examples... Bohr from out of philosophical smoke clouds constantly searching for the tools to crush one example after the other."

He is close friends with both combatants. He stays at Einstein's house; he stays at Bohr's. He cannot choose between them because he understands both positions and finds neither fully satisfying. The new quantum mechanics is moving faster than he can follow, and he knows it.

Six years from now, on 25 September 1933, Ehrenfest will shoot his youngest son Wassik — who has Down syndrome — and then himself, in a park in Amsterdam. His suicide note is addressed to Bohr, Einstein, and the other friends from this circle. He writes that he can no longer keep up with physics, that the gap between what he understands and what the field demands has become unbearable.

In the photograph from this conference, he stands in the back row, looking alert and engaged. He has six years left.

Paul Langevin (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Paul Langevin—7)

Person

October 1927. Langevin is 55. He studied under Pierre Curie for his doctorate and under J.J. Thomson at the Cavendish. His work on paramagnetism and diamagnetism is well established. During the war he developed ultrasonic submarine detection — practical physics saving lives.

He sits in the front row next to Marie Curie. Their relationship is complicated — the affair between them in 1910-11, after Pierre's death, became a public scandal that nearly cost Curie her second Nobel. Sixteen years later, they are colleagues and friends, the scandal long past.

When Lorentz dies next February, Langevin will succeed him as president of the Solvay conferences — a role he will hold through increasingly dark times. In 1940 the Nazis will arrest him. He will become one of France's most prominent anti-fascist intellectuals. After the war, his remains will be transferred to the Panthéon alongside those of the Curies.

In October 1927, all of that is ahead. He is a bridge — between experiment and theory, between the French and Germanic physics communities, between the generation of Curie and the generation of de Broglie.

Peter Debye—7

Person

Peter Debye (1884–1966). Dutch-American physicist and physical chemist. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1936 for contributions to the study of molecular structure through dipole moments and X-ray diffraction.

Developed the Debye model of specific heat in solids (extending Einstein's earlier model) and the Debye-Huckel theory of electrolyte solutions. Born in Maastricht, he held professorships across Europe before emigrating to the United States in 1940 to chair the chemistry department at Cornell University.

Ralph Fowler (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Ralph Fowler—7)

Person

October 1927. Fowler is 38. He is Dirac's doctoral supervisor — the man who introduced the quiet young engineer from Bristol to quantum mechanics. He is also Rutherford's son-in-law, having married Eileen Rutherford in 1921.

Fowler is the bridge between British physics and the continental quantum revolution. Cambridge has the Cavendish Laboratory, the greatest experimental physics laboratory in the world, but it has been slower to embrace quantum theory than Copenhagen or Göttingen. Fowler is the one who brings the new ideas across the Channel, who reads the German papers and explains them to his students.

He will supervise 64 doctoral students in total — an extraordinary number. Dirac, sitting near him in the middle row, is the most brilliant of them, but far from the only one who owes his career to Fowler's guidance and connections.

His wife Eileen will die in 1930. Fowler himself will die in 1944, at 55 — too young, and before many of the physicists he trained have reached the peak of their influence. In October 1927 he is the connector, the enabler, the man who makes it possible for a Bristol engineer's son to sit among the greatest physicists in the world.

Theophile de Donder—7

Person

Théophile de Donder (1872–1957). Belgian mathematician and physicist. Professor at the Free University of Brussels. Considered the father of irreversible thermodynamics — he introduced the concept of chemical affinity as a thermodynamic quantity and developed the theory of chemical thermodynamics that later influenced Ilya Prigogine.

A friend of Einstein, he worked on applications of general relativity to gravitational theory. His major work, /Théorie Nouvelle de la Mécanique Chimique/ (1920), laid the groundwork for non-equilibrium thermodynamics. Largely self-taught, he rose from modest origins to become one of Belgium's leading theoretical scientists.

Werner Heisenberg (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Werner Heisenberg—7)

Person

October 1927. Heisenberg is 25 years old. He published matrix mechanics two years ago — a complete mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics that arrived before Schrödinger's wave equation. The uncertainty principle is six months old, published in March 1927.

He is at the height of his powers, part of what the older generation calls the Knabenphysik — the physics of boys. He, Pauli, Dirac, and Jordan are remaking the foundations of physics while still in their twenties. Bohr is his mentor; Copenhagen is his intellectual home.

At Solvay he watches his mentor debate the most famous scientist in the world — and win. The uncertainty principle, his principle, is at the center of the argument. Einstein attacks it; Bohr defends it. Heisenberg stands with Bohr.

He does not yet know what the next eighteen years will bring: the Nobel Prize at 31, the decision to stay in Germany under the Nazis, the uranium project, the ambiguity that will haunt his reputation forever. In October 1927 he is simply brilliant, young, and certain that the universe is uncertain.

He stands in the back row of the photograph, far right, looking like what he is: a young man who has just changed the world and knows it.

William Lawrence Bragg—7

Person

William Lawrence Bragg (1890–1971). Australian-born British physicist. Nobel Prize in Physics 1915 (shared with his father William Henry Bragg) for their work on X-ray crystallography — at age 25 he remains the youngest Nobel laureate in Physics.

Formulated Bragg's law, which describes the angles at which X-rays are diffracted by crystal lattice planes. Later directed the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge, where he oversaw the work that led to the discovery of the structure of DNA. Founded the field of X-ray crystallography as a practical tool for determining molecular structure.

Wolfgang Pauli (Solvay 1927)—7expression(← Wolfgang Pauli—7)

Person

October 1927. Pauli is 27. His exclusion principle is two years old. He is already feared.

His godfather was Ernst Mach — the philosopher- physicist whose name is on the speed of sound. He studied under Sommerfeld, who set him the task of writing a review of general relativity as a graduate student. The resulting monograph, published when Pauli was 21, was so good that Einstein praised it.

At this conference, Pauli is the executioner. When de Broglie presents his pilot-wave theory — a deterministic alternative to Copenhagen — Pauli dismantles it with a specific technical objection that de Broglie cannot answer. The theory dies in the room. De Broglie abandons it for twenty-five years. Whether Pauli's objection was actually fatal (Bohm will show in 1952 that it wasn't) or merely persuasive is one of the great what-ifs in the history of physics.

Pauli's cruelty is legendary. "Not even wrong" is his dismissal for theories too vague to be tested. He reduces grown physicists to silence. But his judgment is almost always right, which makes it worse.

He stands in the back row of the photograph, next to Heisenberg — friends and rivals since their student days in Munich. The Knabenphysik in full force: two men in their mid-twenties who have already changed physics and are not finished.

International Solvay Institutes—13

Organization

Founded in 1912 by Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay. Based in Brussels. Organizes the Solvay Conferences on Physics and Chemistry — invitation-only gatherings of leading scientists to discuss open problems.

The first conference (1911), organized by Lorentz at Solvay's request, brought together Planck, Einstein, Curie, Rutherford, and Poincaré to discuss "The Theory of Radiation and Quanta." The tradition continues to the present day.
