Notes

October 1927. Born is 44. He co-presents with Heisenberg on matrix mechanics at Lorentz's request. His probabilistic interpretation of the wave function — the Born rule, published just last year — is one of the pillars being debated.

The Born rule says the wave function gives you probabilities, not certainties. The square of the amplitude tells you the likelihood of finding a particle in a particular state. This is the idea Einstein cannot accept. "God does not play dice," Einstein tells Born directly. They are personal friends. The disagreement will last the rest of their lives.

Born supervised both Heisenberg and Pauli at Göttingen. The matrix mechanics that Heisenberg published in 1925 grew directly from Born's research program. Born is the senior figure, the institutional anchor — but it is his students who get the glory. Heisenberg will win the Nobel in 1932 alone, without Born. The Nobel committee will not recognize the Born rule until 1954 — twenty-eight years after he proposed it, three years after Einstein's death.

In October 1927 he is at the peak of his contributions. The recognition will take decades to catch up.
