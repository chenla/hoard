Notes

October 1927. Compton is 35. He is about to share the Nobel Prize with C.T.R. Wilson — the announcement comes just weeks after this conference.

The Compton effect, discovered in 1922-23, is one of the experimental facts driving everything being debated in this room. He showed that X-rays scattering off electrons behave exactly as particle collisions — transferring momentum in discrete quanta. This was direct, inescapable evidence for the particle nature of light, alongside the wave interference patterns that were equally well established.

Wave-particle duality — the central theme of this conference, titled "Electrons and Photons" — exists as a scientific problem partly because of Compton's results. Bohr's complementarity, Heisenberg's uncertainty, de Broglie's matter waves — all are attempts to make sense of a world where Compton's particles and Young's interference fringes are both undeniably real.

He does not yet know that he will later chair the committee that launches the Manhattan Project's plutonium program. In October 1927, he is an experimentalist whose data has forced the theorists to rethink reality.
