** Scope Note

German mathematician (1862–1943).  Posed the 23 problems
that shaped twentieth-century mathematics.  His
formalization program — the attempt to ground all
mathematics on a complete, consistent, decidable
foundation — was the mathematical equivalent of the
deterministic worldview.  Gödel destroyed it in 1931.

** Notes

David Hilbert (1862–1943).  Professor at the University
of Göttingen, which under his leadership became the
world center for mathematics.  His 1900 address to the
International Congress of Mathematicians posed 23
problems that set the research agenda for the century —
several remain unsolved.

His program to formalize mathematics (1920s) was the
last great attempt to save the deterministic vision in
the mathematical domain.  If successful, every
mathematical question would have a definitive answer
derivable from axioms.  Von Neumann was among his most
brilliant champions.  Gödel's incompleteness theorems
(1931) showed all three goals — completeness,
consistency, decidability — were unachievable
simultaneously.

His epitaph reads "Wir müssen wissen.  Wir werden
wissen." (We must know.  We shall know.) — spoken in a
radio address just days before Gödel presented his
incompleteness theorems at the Königsberg conference.
The timing is almost unbearable: the most confident
assertion of the deterministic program, delivered at the
precise moment of its destruction.

Hilbert's Göttingen was not just a mathematics
department — it was the center of the mathematical
world.  He attracted and trained many of the figures
who appear elsewhere in the Solvay story: von Neumann,
Born, Heisenberg, Courant, Weyl.  The intellectual
culture he created — rigorous, ambitious, convinced
that mathematics could provide certain foundations for
all of science — was the environment that produced both
quantum mechanics and the tools that dismantled its
deterministic assumptions.

His 23 problems, posed at the 1900 International
Congress of Mathematicians in Paris, were a declaration
that mathematics had a knowable frontier — that every
important question could be stated precisely and, in
principle, answered.  The second problem (prove the
consistency of arithmetic) and the tenth (find an
algorithm to solve all Diophantine equations) were
answered in the negative by Gödel and by Matiyasevich
respectively.  The program of formalization he pursued
in the 1920s — completeness, consistency, decidability
— was the mathematical expression of the same
deterministic confidence that animated classical
physics.  Its destruction by Gödel, Turing, and Church
in the 1930s was the mathematical parallel to the
quantum revolution: the universe refuses to be fully
captured by formal systems, whether physical or logical.

Hilbert lived until 1943, long enough to see the Nazis
destroy his department — when asked by a Nazi official
how mathematics at Göttingen was faring "now that it
has been freed of the Jewish influence," he replied:
"Mathematics at Göttingen?  There is really none any
more."

*** Key Works

**** Before 1927

- /Grundlagen der Geometrie/ (1899).  Foundations of
  Geometry — rebuilt Euclidean geometry on a rigorous
  axiomatic basis, the model for all subsequent
  axiomatization.
- 23 Problems (1900).  The research agenda for
  twentieth-century mathematics, posed at the
  International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris.
- Hilbert spaces (1900s–1910s).  The infinite-
  dimensional vector spaces that became the
  mathematical framework for quantum mechanics.

**** After 1927

- Hilbert's program (1920s–1931).  The attempt to prove
  the consistency and completeness of mathematics
  from finitary methods — destroyed by Gödel's
  incompleteness theorems in 1931.
- /Grundlagen der Mathematik/ (with Bernays, 1934–1939).
  The two-volume attempt to salvage what could be
  saved of the formalization program after Gödel.
