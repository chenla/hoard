** Notes

Wolfgang Pauli (1900–1958).  Austrian-Swiss
theoretical physicist.  Nobel Prize in Physics 1945
for the discovery of the exclusion principle (1925),
which states that no two fermions can occupy the same
quantum state simultaneously — a cornerstone of
atomic structure and the periodic table.

Predicted the existence of the neutrino in 1930 to
explain the continuous energy spectrum of beta decay.
Godson of Ernst Mach.  Feared throughout physics for
his withering criticism — his dismissal "not even
wrong" became legendary.  At 27 he was one of the
younger participants at the 1927 Solvay Conference,
already recognized as one of the sharpest minds in
theoretical physics.

Pauli played a decisive role at the 1927 Solvay
Conference.  When Louis de Broglie presented his
pilot-wave theory — a deterministic alternative to
the Copenhagen interpretation — it was Pauli who
demolished it.  His critique was so devastating that
de Broglie abandoned the theory for the next quarter
century, and pilot-wave approaches were not seriously
revived until David Bohm's reformulation in 1952.
History judges this harshly: Bohm's version proved
viable, and Pauli may have delayed a legitimate
research program by decades.  But in the moment, his
intervention helped cement the Copenhagen consensus.

His exclusion principle (1925) is one of those results
that sounds technical but explains nearly everything:
why matter takes up space, why the periodic table has
its structure, why stars can resist gravitational
collapse.  Without it, all electrons would drop to the
lowest energy state and chemistry would not exist.  The
principle earned him the Nobel Prize in 1945, though by
then he had already made half a dozen other first-rank
contributions.

His later work with Villars on regularization and the
spin-statistics theorem (connecting particle spin to
whether particles obey Fermi-Dirac or Bose-Einstein
statistics) continued to shape the mathematical
foundations of quantum field theory.  He also engaged
in a long and unusual correspondence with Carl Jung
about the relationship between physics and psychology
— a side of Pauli that surprised colleagues who knew
him only as the field's most merciless critic.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- /Exclusion principle/ (1925).  No two fermions can
  occupy the same quantum state — the principle that
  explains atomic structure and the periodic table.
- Pauli matrices (1927).  The mathematical representation
  of spin-1/2 particles, foundational to quantum
  mechanics.
- Spin theory (1924–1927).  Developed the non-relativistic
  theory of electron spin.

**** After Solvay

- Neutrino prediction (1930).  Postulated an undetected
  neutral particle to explain the continuous energy
  spectrum of beta decay — confirmed experimentally by
  Cowan and Reines in 1956.
- Spin-statistics theorem (1940).  Proved that particles
  with integer spin are bosons and particles with
  half-integer spin are fermions — a deep connection
  between quantum mechanics and special relativity.
- Pauli-Villars regularization (1949).  A technique for
  handling infinities in quantum field theory
  calculations.
