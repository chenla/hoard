** Notes

October 1927.  Wilson is 58.  He is about to win
the Nobel Prize — announced just weeks after this
conference, shared with Arthur Compton, who sits
nearby in the middle row.

His cloud chamber, invented in 1911, made the
invisible visible.  For the first time, you could
see the tracks of individual particles — alpha
particles, electrons, scattered photons.  The
theoretical debates swirling around this conference
are about particles and waves; Wilson built the
instrument that shows you the particles are real.

The cloud chamber started from an unlikely place:
Wilson was a meteorologist fascinated by the optical
phenomena of clouds on Scottish mountains.  He built
a chamber to study cloud formation and discovered
it could detect ionizing radiation.  The beautiful
photographs of particle tracks it produced became
essential evidence in the quantum debates.

He is the experimentalist among theoreticians —
quiet, Scottish, methodical.  While Bohr and Einstein
argue about whether God plays dice, Wilson's cloud
chamber shows you the dice landing.
