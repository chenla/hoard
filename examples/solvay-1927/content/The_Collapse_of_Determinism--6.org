** Scope Note

How the uncertainty that shattered physics at Solvay
propagated through mathematics — Cantor's infinities,
Hilbert's program, Russell's Principia, Gödel's
incompleteness — until the deterministic dream was
dead in every formal domain.

** Notes

The week in Brussels settled the physics.  Copenhagen
won.  The universe was probabilistic, measurement was
irreversible, and the clockwork was broken.

But physics was only the first domino.

The same structural problem — that a system cannot be
both complete and consistent — appeared everywhere
people looked.  In mathematics.  In logic.  In
computation.  Each collapse was independent, proved by
different people using different methods, and yet they
all said the same thing: the deterministic dream was
not merely difficult.  It was impossible.

---

*** Cantor's Crack

The trouble began before Solvay.  In 1891, Georg Cantor
proved with his diagonal argument that the real numbers
are uncountably infinite — strictly larger than the
natural numbers. @@margin:Georg_Cantor@@  Infinity was not
one thing but many.  The mathematical universe was wilder
and stranger than anyone had imagined.

Leopold Kronecker attacked Cantor's work as mathematical
pathology.  Hilbert defended it as "the finest product
of mathematical genius."  Cantor spent his final years in
a sanatorium. @@margin:Cantors_Infinities@@

The cracks Cantor exposed — paradoxes in the foundations
of set theory, including Russell's paradox — demanded a
response.

*** The Monuments

Two responses were attempted simultaneously.

Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead spent over a
decade building /Principia Mathematica/ — three volumes,
362 pages to prove that 1+1=2 — to derive all of
mathematics from pure logic. @@margin:Bertrand_Russell@@
@@margin:Principia_Mathematica@@  The greatest formal
system ever constructed.

David Hilbert proposed something even more ambitious: a
program to formalize /all/ of mathematics on a complete,
consistent, decidable foundation. @@margin:David_Hilbert@@
@@margin:Hilberts_Program@@  If it worked, mathematics
would be as certain as Newton's clockwork had once
seemed.  The young John von Neumann was an early
champion. @@margin:John_von_Neumann@@

*** Gödel's Hammer

In 1931, Kurt Gödel published two theorems that destroyed
both projects in 26 pages. @@margin:Kurt_Godel@@
@@margin:Incompleteness_Theorems@@

The first theorem: any consistent formal system powerful
enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot
prove.  The system is necessarily incomplete.

The second theorem: such a system cannot prove its own
consistency.

Von Neumann proved the second theorem independently and
recognized the implications immediately.  Hilbert's
program was dead.  So was the ambition behind Principia.
Mathematics could not be both complete and consistent.
The deterministic dream — that everything could be known,
formalized, decided — was impossible in principle, not
merely in practice.

The mathematical parallel to Heisenberg's uncertainty was
exact.  Different domain, same structural result.

*** Einstein's Last Stand

Einstein never accepted Copenhagen.  In 1935 he published
the EPR paper with Podolsky and Rosen — a thought
experiment arguing that quantum mechanics must be
incomplete because it implied instantaneous correlations
between distant particles. @@margin:EPR_Paradox@@
"Spooky action at a distance."

Bohr responded within months, rejecting the premise.  The
debate appeared to be philosophical — a matter of taste,
not experiment.  It stayed that way for nearly thirty
years.

*** Bell's Resolution

In 1964, working largely in his spare time at CERN, John
Bell proved a theorem that transformed the debate from
philosophy into experimental physics.
@@margin:John_Bell@@ @@margin:Bells_Theorem@@

Bell derived an inequality that any local realist theory
must satisfy.  Quantum mechanics predicts violations of
this inequality for entangled particles.  The question
Einstein raised at breakfast at Solvay in 1927 was finally
experimentally testable.

Alain Aspect's 1982 experiments confirmed the violation.
The result is stark: either locality is false — particles
can be correlated instantaneously regardless of distance —
or realism is false — particles do not have definite
properties before measurement.  Or both.

The answer was not what Einstein hoped.

---

By the 1960s the deterministic paradigm was dead in
physics and in mathematics.  Heisenberg had shown the
physical limits.  Gödel had shown the logical limits.
Bell had shown the experimental confirmation.

What remained was to build with the ruins.  And that
is what happened next.
