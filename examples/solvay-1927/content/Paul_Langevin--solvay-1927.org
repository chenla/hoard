** Notes

October 1927.  Langevin is 55.  He studied under
Pierre Curie for his doctorate and under J.J. Thomson
at the Cavendish.  His work on paramagnetism and
diamagnetism is well established.  During the war he
developed ultrasonic submarine detection — practical
physics saving lives.

He sits in the front row next to Marie Curie.  Their
relationship is complicated — the affair between them
in 1910-11, after Pierre's death, became a public
scandal that nearly cost Curie her second Nobel.
Sixteen years later, they are colleagues and friends,
the scandal long past.

When Lorentz dies next February, Langevin will succeed
him as president of the Solvay conferences — a role
he will hold through increasingly dark times.  In
1940 the Nazis will arrest him.  He will become one
of France's most prominent anti-fascist intellectuals.
After the war, his remains will be transferred to the
Panthéon alongside those of the Curies.

In October 1927, all of that is ahead.  He is a
bridge — between experiment and theory, between the
French and Germanic physics communities, between the
generation of Curie and the generation of de Broglie.
