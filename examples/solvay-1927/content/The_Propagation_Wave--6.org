** Scope Note

From the collapse outward: Manhattan Project, the
transistor, the stored-program computer, neural networks,
AlphaGo's Move 37, and the arrival of transformer-based
AI.  The shockwave from Solvay reaches the present.

** Notes

The deterministic worldview was dead.  Physics had
proved it at Solvay.  Mathematics had proved it with
Gödel.  Experiments would prove it with Bell.

But the ruins were fertile.

The people who built the next world — the bomb, the
computer, the network, the learning machine — were the
same people, or the students of the same people, who
had watched the old certainties collapse.  They built
with probability because that was what was left.

---

*** Von Neumann's Bridge

John von Neumann is the hinge.  He was at Solvay.  He
formalized quantum mechanics.  He proved the second
incompleteness theorem.  And then he turned from
foundations to machines. @@margin:John_von_Neumann@@

When Hilbert's program collapsed, von Neumann did not
mourn.  He pivoted.  If you could not derive all truth
from axioms, you could at least compute with what you
had.  The stored-program computer — instructions and data
in the same memory, programs that modify themselves — was
his answer. @@margin:Stored_Program_Computer@@

His IAS machine at Princeton, which he called the
MANIAC, was one of the first implementations.
@@margin:The_MANIAC_Computer@@

*** The Bomb

Before the computer came the bomb.  The Manhattan
Project was the first industrial application of quantum
physics — turning the abstract probabilities debated
at Solvay into the most destructive force in history.
@@margin:Manhattan_Project@@

Von Neumann designed the implosion lens that made the
plutonium bomb possible.  Many Solvay attendees or their
students were involved.  The physics that made it work —
fission, quantum tunneling, neutron cross-sections — was
the direct offspring of the quantum revolution.

The Trinity test demonstrated that quantum mechanics
was not merely a philosophical puzzle.  It could
reshape the physical world.

*** Formalizing Information

In parallel, two other threads were being woven.

Alan Turing formalized computation itself.  His 1936
paper defined the Turing machine and proved the halting
problem undecidable — the computational echo of Gödel's
incompleteness.  If mathematics could not decide all
propositions, neither could any machine.
@@margin:Alan_Turing@@

Claude Shannon formalized information.  His 1948 paper
created information theory — defining entropy, channel
capacity, and the bit.  He showed that information
could be quantified and transmitted reliably through
noisy channels. @@margin:Claude_Shannon@@  He made the
probabilistic nature of communication mathematically
precise.

Between them, Turing and Shannon built the conceptual
infrastructure for everything that followed.

*** Silicon

The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, was the
physical bridge. @@margin:Transistor@@  A
quantum-mechanical switch — its operation depends on
band theory, which is pure quantum mechanics applied to
solid-state materials.

Without quantum theory there would be no understanding
of semiconductors.  No transistors.  No integrated
circuits.  No microprocessors.  The device that made the
digital revolution possible is, at its foundation, a
quantum machine.

*** Learning Machines

McCulloch and Pitts proposed the first mathematical model
of a neural network in 1943.  Rosenblatt built the
perceptron in 1958.  Minsky and Papert's critique killed
funding for a generation.  Backpropagation was
rediscovered in the 1980s.  Deep learning scaled with
GPUs and data. @@margin:Neural_Networks@@

These are probabilistic machines.  They learn statistical
patterns from data rather than following explicit
instructions.  They are, in a precise sense, the opposite
of the deterministic clockwork Laplace imagined.

*** Move 37

In March 2016, AlphaGo played Lee Sedol in Seoul.
@@margin:Lee_Sedol@@  Go was supposed to be the game
machines couldn't play — its branching factor made
brute-force search impossible.  You needed something
closer to intuition.

In game two, move 37, AlphaGo placed a stone on the
fifth line that no human would have played.
@@margin:AlphaGo_Move_37@@  The commentators were
confused.  Lee Sedol left the room.  When he returned,
he had realized the move was brilliant.

Demis Hassabis, watching from the control room, said
his team's reaction was the same as Lee's — they didn't
understand the move either.  The machine had found
something none of its creators could explain.
@@margin:Demis_Hassabis@@

This was the moment the shockwave from Solvay reached
the general public.  A machine that learned from
probability had surpassed human judgment in a domain
that was supposed to require human intuition.

---

The shockwave is still propagating.

Transformer-based language models — machines that
predict the next token from statistical patterns in
text — are the latest wavefront.  They write, they
reason, they code.  They are wrong in ways no human
would be, and right in ways no human could be.

Von Neumann, dying of cancer at 53, whispered that a
thinking machine "would have to grow, not be built...
understand language... and play, like a child."

It took sixty years.  But the machines are playing now.

And the twenty-nine people on the steps of the Institut
International de Physique Solvay, Brussels, October
1927 — they are where this story begins.
