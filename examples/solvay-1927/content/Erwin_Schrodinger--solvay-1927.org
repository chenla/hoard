** Notes

October 1927.  Schrödinger is 40.  His wave equation
is barely a year old — published in 1926 in a burst
of four papers that arrived like a thunderclap.  He
formalized de Broglie's wave hypothesis into a
rigorous mathematical framework.  The equation works
beautifully.  The question is what it means.

Schrödinger wants it to mean that particles are
actually waves — that the wave function describes a
real, physical field.  Born says no: the wave
function gives you probabilities, not physical waves.
Heisenberg's matrix mechanics gives the same answers
through a completely different formalism.  Dirac has
just shown the two are mathematically equivalent.

Schrödinger sides with Einstein and de Broglie
against the Copenhagen interpretation.  He finds
Born's probabilistic reading of his own equation
deeply unsatisfying.  Years later he will invent the
famous cat thought experiment (1935) precisely to
expose the absurdity of quantum superposition taken
literally.

At the conference he represents one half of the
mathematical toolkit — wave mechanics versus
Heisenberg's matrix mechanics.  The mathematics is
settled; the meaning is not.  He will share the
Nobel with Dirac in 1933, flee Austria in 1938,
and spend the rest of his career in Dublin, still
arguing that the wave function should be taken
literally.

He never comes to terms with Born's interpretation.
Neither does Einstein.  The universe does not appear
to care.
