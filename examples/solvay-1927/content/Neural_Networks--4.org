** Scope Note

Computational models inspired by biological neurons.
From McCulloch-Pitts (1943) through Rosenblatt's
perceptron (1958) to deep learning and transformers.
Machines that learn from probability rather than execute
from certainty — the computational embodiment of the
post-deterministic paradigm.

** Notes

Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed the first
mathematical model of a neural network in 1943, showing
that networks of simple threshold units could compute
any logical function.  Frank Rosenblatt built the
perceptron in 1958 — the first machine that could learn
from examples.  Progress stalled after Marvin Minsky
and Seymour Papert's 1969 critique showed the
perceptron's limitations, and funding dried up for over
a decade.

Backpropagation was rediscovered in the 1980s by
Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, enabling multi-layer
networks to learn.  Deep learning scaled the approach
with GPUs and massive datasets — Geoffrey Hinton, Yann
LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio drove this revolution and
shared the 2018 Turing Award.  Convolutional networks
conquered vision; recurrent networks tackled sequences.

AlphaGo (2016) and transformer-based language models
(2017–present) represent the culmination: machines that
learn statistical patterns from data rather than follow
explicit instructions.  They are, in a precise sense,
probabilistic machines — the opposite of the
deterministic clockwork that Laplace imagined.  The
shockwave from Solvay, propagating through Gödel and
Turing and von Neumann, arrives here: machines that
embrace uncertainty as their operating principle.

The history is punctuated by AI winters — periods when
funding and interest collapsed.  The first followed the
Minsky-Papert critique (1969); the second came in the
late 1980s when expert systems failed to deliver on
their promises.  Each time, the field was rescued by
the same insight: more data and more compute could
overcome theoretical limitations that seemed
fundamental.  The pattern itself is instructive — the
deterministic approach (symbolic AI, rule-based
systems, expert systems) kept failing; the
probabilistic approach (neural networks, statistical
learning) kept reviving.

Neural networks are fundamentally probabilistic
machines.  They do not learn rules; they learn
statistical patterns.  A trained network does not
"know" what a cat looks like in any way that can be
extracted as a set of explicit propositions — it has
adjusted millions of numerical weights until its
outputs correlate with its training data.  This is
not a bug or a limitation.  It is the design.  These
are machines that work /by/ probability rather than
/despite/ it — the first engineered systems that
operate on the same principle the universe was shown
to operate on at Solvay.

The transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017)
is the current wavefront.  Attention mechanisms allow
the network to learn which parts of a sequence are
relevant to which other parts — a fundamentally
relational and contextual computation.  The resulting
models (GPT, Claude, Gemini) demonstrate capabilities
that no one predicted from their architecture.  They
are, in a sense, the computational proof that
probabilistic foundations can produce results that
deterministic systems never could.
