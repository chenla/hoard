** Scope Note

British AI researcher and entrepreneur (b. 1976).
Co-founded DeepMind in 2010.  Built AlphaGo, which
defeated Lee Sedol in 2016 — the moment the shockwave
from Solvay reached the general public.  AlphaFold
solved protein structure prediction.  Nobel Prize in
Chemistry 2024.

** Notes

Demis Hassabis (b. 1976).  Chess prodigy — second-highest
rated player in the world for his age at thirteen.
Designed the video game Theme Park at seventeen for
Bullfrog Productions.  Earned a PhD in cognitive
neuroscience at UCL, studying the neural basis of
imagination and memory.

Co-founded DeepMind in 2010 with Shane Legg and Mustafa
Suleyman, with the explicit goal of solving artificial
general intelligence.  Google acquired DeepMind in 2014
for approximately £400 million.

AlphaGo's victory over Lee Sedol in March 2016 was a
watershed moment.  Go's complexity — more possible board
positions than atoms in the observable universe — made
brute-force search impossible.  AlphaGo had to learn
through pattern recognition and self-play, making it the
first time a machine surpassed human performance at a
task that required what humans call intuition.  Move 37
in Game 2 was the iconic moment: a move no human would
play, which turned out to be brilliant.

AlphaFold (2020) predicted protein structures with
experimental accuracy, solving a fifty-year-old problem
in biology.  It was arguably the most significant
scientific application of AI to date.  Hassabis shared
the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

What connects Hassabis to the Solvay narrative is the
arc from games to science.  Chess, Go, and protein
folding are all problems where the search space exceeds
brute-force computation — they require something that
resembles judgment.  Hassabis's insight, rooted in his
neuroscience training, was that the same learning
architectures that evolved in biological brains could
be reimplemented in silicon.  AlphaGo did not play Go
the way humans play Go, but it played it better.
AlphaFold did not reason about chemistry the way
chemists reason, but it predicted structures more
accurately.  The implications extend far beyond any
single domain.

His PhD work at UCL on the hippocampus and episodic
memory was not a detour from AI — it was the
theoretical foundation.  He studied how the brain
constructs imagined scenes from fragments of memory,
and this research on generative models of experience
fed directly into DeepMind's architecture.  The
connection between imagination, memory, and prediction
is not metaphorical; it is the computational principle
underlying both biological and artificial intelligence.

DeepMind under Hassabis represents a particular vision
of AI development: that artificial general intelligence
is best approached through neuroscience-informed
architectures applied to progressively harder problems.
Whether this vision holds or is overtaken by scaling
approaches remains an open question.  But AlphaGo's
Move 37 — the move no human would play — already
demonstrated something that the participants at Solvay
would have recognized: the boundary between human
understanding and something stranger had been crossed
again.
