** Scope Note

David Hilbert's 1920s program to formalize all of
mathematics on a complete, consistent, decidable
foundation.  The most ambitious attempt to save
determinism in mathematics — destroyed by Gödel in
1931.

** Notes

Hilbert proposed three goals for the foundations of
mathematics: that all mathematics could be reduced to a
finite set of axioms (formalization), that the system
could be proven consistent within itself (consistency),
and that every mathematical proposition could be decided
as true or false (decidability).

John von Neumann was an early and enthusiastic champion
of the program.  He was working on consistency proofs
when Gödel presented his incompleteness theorems in
September 1930.  Von Neumann immediately grasped the
implications — he independently proved the second
incompleteness theorem and abandoned his own line of
research.

Von Neumann's role is telling.  He was perhaps the
only person at the time with the technical range to
see both the power of Hilbert's vision and the
totality of its collapse.  When he abandoned his
consistency proofs, he did not retreat into despair —
he pivoted.  The same formal machinery that Hilbert
had hoped would secure mathematics became, in von
Neumann's hands, the architecture of the stored-
program computer.  The failure of the program opened
the door to computation: if not all mathematical
truths can be decided by proof, perhaps they can be
explored by machine.  Turing made this explicit in
1936, but von Neumann had already sensed the
direction.

The program's failure forced mathematics to accept the
same kind of fundamental uncertainty that physics had
encountered at Solvay.  Complete knowledge was
impossible — not as a practical limitation but as a
structural feature of formal systems themselves.
Hilbert's epitaph, "Wir mussen wissen.  Wir werden
wissen" (We must know.  We shall know), became an ironic
monument to the deterministic dream.
