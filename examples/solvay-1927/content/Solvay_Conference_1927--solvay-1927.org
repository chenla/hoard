** Notes

October 1927.  Twenty-nine physicists gather in Brussels
for the Fifth Solvay Conference.  The official theme is
"Electrons and Photons."  The real subject is whether the
universe is a clockwork.

By 1927, the evidence is overwhelming.  Planck's quantum
of action (1900) shattered the continuity of energy.
Einstein's photoelectric effect (1905) showed light
behaving as particles.  Bohr's atomic model (1913) worked
but made no classical sense.  De Broglie proposed that
particles have wavelengths (1924).  Heisenberg built
matrix mechanics (1925).  Schrödinger wrote his wave
equation (1926).  Born gave it a probabilistic
interpretation.  And now Heisenberg has published the
uncertainty principle — not as a practical limitation
on measurement, but as a fundamental feature of nature.

The stage is set.  On one side: Bohr, Heisenberg, Born,
Pauli, Dirac — the Copenhagen faction, arguing that
probability is intrinsic, that asking what a particle
"really does" between measurements is meaningless.  On
the other: Einstein, Schrödinger, de Broglie, Lorentz
— insisting that there must be a deeper reality beneath
the statistics.

Einstein does not present a paper.  He attacks from the
floor, devising thought experiments to expose what he
sees as the incompleteness of the quantum formalism.
Each morning at breakfast he proposes a new objection.
Each evening Bohr refutes it.  Ehrenfest, close to both
men, moves between them like a shuttle in a loom — and
weeps at the impasse.

De Broglie presents his pilot-wave theory — a
deterministic alternative in which particles follow
definite trajectories guided by a wave field.  Pauli
demolishes it in the discussion.  De Broglie abandons
the idea for twenty-five years.  (Bohm will reconstruct
it in 1952, answering Pauli's objection.  The question
was never settled.)

The Copenhagen interpretation carries the day.  But
Einstein never accepts it.  He will spend the rest of
his life constructing increasingly sophisticated
objections — the EPR paradox, the critique of
nonlocality — that will not be experimentally tested
until decades after his death.

What happened in Brussels in October 1927 was not a
resolution.  It was a rupture.  The deterministic
worldview that had governed Western science, governance,
and economics since Newton cracked open and never closed
again.  The shockwave from that week propagated outward
through the rest of the century and into ours — through
Gödel, through Turing, through the bomb, through the
transistor, through Bell's theorem, through the neural
network that learned to play Go better than any human
being.

The photograph captures the moment.  The shockwave is
still propagating.
