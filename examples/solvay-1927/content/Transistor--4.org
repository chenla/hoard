** Scope Note

Invented at Bell Labs in 1947 by Bardeen, Brattain, and
Shockley.  A quantum-mechanical switch — its operation
depends on the band theory of solids, which is a direct
consequence of quantum mechanics.  The physical device
that turned the probabilistic theory debated at Solvay
into the substrate of computation.

** Notes

The point-contact transistor was demonstrated on
December 23, 1947, by John Bardeen and Walter Brattain
at Bell Labs, under the supervision of William Shockley.
Shockley developed the junction transistor shortly
after.  All three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in
Physics.

The transistor's physics — electron tunneling,
semiconductor band gaps, doping — is pure quantum
mechanics applied to solid-state materials.  The band
theory of solids, developed in the late 1920s and 1930s
by Felix Bloch, Rudolf Peierls, and Alan Wilson,
explains why some materials conduct electricity, some
insulate, and some can be made to switch between the
two.  Without this theory — which is a direct
application of quantum mechanics to crystalline
structures — there would be no understanding of
semiconductors.

The transistor replaced vacuum tubes, enabling smaller,
faster, and vastly more reliable electronic devices.
It led to the integrated circuit (1958), the
microprocessor (1971), and everything that followed.
The device that made the digital revolution possible
is, at its foundation, a quantum machine.

Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley shared the 1956 Nobel
Prize in Physics — but the collaboration was toxic.
Shockley had wanted credit for the invention and was
furious that Bardeen and Brattain had succeeded
without him.  He developed the junction transistor
partly to establish his own claim, then left Bell Labs
to found Shockley Semiconductor in Palo Alto.  His
management was so abusive that eight of his best
researchers left to found Fairchild Semiconductor in
1957 — the company that spawned Intel, AMD, and the
entire Silicon Valley ecosystem.  The transistor's
social history is as consequential as its physics.

The technical point that matters for the Screed arc is
this: the transistor is a quantum device.  Its
operation depends on quantum mechanics in a way that
is not incidental but fundamental.  Without quantum
theory there would be no band theory of solids — the
framework, developed by Bloch, Peierls, and Wilson in
the late 1920s and 1930s, that explains why some
materials conduct, some insulate, and some can be made
to switch.  Without band theory there would be no
understanding of semiconductors.  Without
semiconductors there would be no doping — the precise
introduction of impurities to create regions of excess
electrons (n-type) or holes (p-type).  Without doping
there would be no p-n junctions, and without p-n
junctions there would be no transistors.

The entire digital revolution — every computer, every
phone, every server, every GPU training every neural
network — rests on quantum physics applied to silicon.
The probabilistic theory that Einstein resisted and
Bohr defended at Solvay is not an abstract
philosophical position.  It is the physical substrate
of the modern world.  Every time a transistor switches,
it enacts quantum mechanics.  There are roughly ten
billion transistors on a modern chip, switching
billions of times per second.  The clockwork universe
is dead, and its replacement runs on quantum switches.
