** Scope Note

Stewart Brand's model of civilization as six layers
moving at different speeds: fashion, commerce,
infrastructure, governance, culture, nature.  Fast layers
innovate; slow layers stabilize.  The whole system works
because the layers are coupled but not synchronized —
change propagates, it does not arrive everywhere at once.

** Notes

Brand introduced pace layers in /The Clock of the Long
Now/ (1999), drawing on Frank Duffy's "shearing layers"
concept for buildings (structure, skin, services, space
plan, stuff) from /How Buildings Learn/ (1994).

The key insight is not the layers themselves but their
interaction.  Fast layers propose; slow layers dispose.
Fashion can try anything because commerce will filter
it.  Commerce can experiment because infrastructure
absorbs the failures.  Each layer learns at its own
rate, and the whole system's resilience comes from this
differential — not from any single layer's stability.

This maps directly onto Hoard's three overlays: Flow
(fast — dynamics, feedback, streams), Structural
(middle — organization, navigation, holons), and Strata
(slow — identity, vocabulary, provenance).  The heap —
unstructured, unsorted material — operates at the
fastest pace of all, the arrival rate of raw experience.

The model also explains why deterministic systems fail:
they assume all layers can be synchronized, that change
at the top can be commanded to propagate uniformly
downward.  But change has momentum, it has locality,
and it propagates at different rates through different
mediums.  Those mediums are, at the end of the day, all
made up of data structures.
