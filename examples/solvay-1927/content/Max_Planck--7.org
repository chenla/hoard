** Scope Note

** Notes

Max Planck (1858–1947).  German theoretical physicist.
Nobel Prize in Physics 1918 for his discovery of energy
quanta.  In 1900 he introduced the quantum of action
(Planck's constant, h), resolving the black-body
radiation problem and inadvertently launching the
quantum revolution.

Professor at the University of Berlin for decades, he
became the elder statesman of German physics.  At the
1927 Solvay Conference he watched the young generation
debate the meaning of the theory his work had made
possible.  He remained philosophically conservative,
never fully comfortable with the Copenhagen
interpretation.

Planck called his own quantum hypothesis "an act of
desperation."  He had not set out to revolutionize
physics — he was trying to solve a specific technical
problem about the spectrum of radiation emitted by
heated bodies.  Classical physics predicted infinite
energy at short wavelengths (the "ultraviolet
catastrophe").  Planck found that the math only worked
if he assumed energy came in discrete packets —
quanta — proportional to frequency.  He spent years
trying to reconcile this with classical physics, hoping
the quantum was a mathematical trick rather than a
physical reality.  It was not.

His personal life was marked by extraordinary loss.
His first wife died in 1909.  His eldest son Karl was
killed at Verdun in 1916.  Both of his daughters died
in childbirth.  He stayed in Germany through the Nazi
era, attempting to preserve what remained of German
science from within the institutions.  He met Hitler
once in 1933 to plead on behalf of Jewish colleagues
and was rebuffed.  His son Erwin was arrested for his
involvement in the July 20, 1944 assassination attempt
against Hitler and executed by the Gestapo in January
1945.  Planck was eighty-six years old.

He died in 1947 in Göttingen, having outlived his
children, his country's scientific institutions, and
the classical worldview he had inadvertently destroyed.
The Max Planck Society, successor to the Kaiser Wilhelm
Society, was named in his honor and remains Germany's
premier research organization.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- Planck's law of black-body radiation (1900).
  Introduced the quantum of action (Planck's constant,
  /h/), resolving the ultraviolet catastrophe.
- Nobel Prize in Physics (1918).  For the discovery
  of energy quanta and its contribution to the
  development of physics.

**** After Solvay

- /The Philosophy of Physics/ (1936).  Philosophical
  reflections on the meaning of the quantum revolution
  he had started.
- Presidency of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society (1930–1937).
  Attempted to preserve German scientific institutions
  under increasingly hostile political conditions.
