** Notes

October 1927.  De Broglie is 35, the 7th Duc de
Broglie — a French aristocrat who chose physics over
the family tradition of diplomacy and politics.  His
doctoral thesis (1924) proposed that all matter has
wave-like properties.  Einstein read it and called it
"a first feeble ray of light on this worst of our
physics enigmas."

Lorentz specifically invited him to present his
pilot-wave theory at this conference.  It is a
deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics —
particles have definite trajectories guided by a
"pilot wave."  It offers an alternative to the
Copenhagen interpretation's embrace of fundamental
randomness.

It does not go well.  Pauli, 27 years old and already
feared for his devastating critiques, attacks the
theory.  De Broglie cannot answer Pauli's objection.
The audience moves on.  Einstein supports de Broglie's
instinct but not his formalism.  Schrödinger's wave
equation was developed partly to put de Broglie's
intuition on rigorous mathematical ground, but the
probabilistic interpretation (Born) has captured it.

De Broglie abandons pilot-wave theory after this
conference.  He will not return to it for twenty-five
years, until David Bohm independently rediscovers and
extends the idea in 1952.  The Nobel committee awards
him the prize in 1929 anyway — for the wave nature of
matter itself, not for the interpretation that was
rejected here.

This conference is where a viable alternative to
Copenhagen was killed.  The road not taken.
