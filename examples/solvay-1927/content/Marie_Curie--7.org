** Scope Note

Polish-French physicist and chemist (1867–1934). Two Nobel Prizes in different sciences — Physics 1903, Chemistry 1911. Pioneer of radioactivity research who discovered polonium and radium. At Solvay, the experimentalist among theoreticians and the only woman in the photograph.

** Notes

Marie Skłodowska-Curie (1867–1934).  Polish-French
physicist and chemist.  Nobel Prize in Physics 1903
(radioactivity, shared with Pierre Curie and Henri
Becquerel).  Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1911 (discovery of
polonium and radium).  First woman to win a Nobel Prize;
only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

Founded the Curie Institutes in Paris and Warsaw.  Died
of aplastic anemia caused by prolonged radiation exposure.
Her laboratory notebooks are still too radioactive to
handle without protective equipment.

At the 1927 Solvay Conference, Curie was the only woman
in the photograph and one of the few experimentalists in
a room dominated by theoreticians.  She had already
survived the scandal of the Langevin affair in 1911,
which nearly cost her the second Nobel, and the grueling
years of operating mobile X-ray units — the /petites
Curies/ — on the Western Front during World War I.  By
1927 she was running the Radium Institute in Paris,
training a generation of researchers in radioactivity.

Her presence at Solvay is a reminder that the quantum
revolution was built on experimental foundations.  The
theoreticians debated interpretations; Curie and her
laboratory had supplied the phenomena they were trying
to interpret.  Radioactive decay — spontaneous,
unpredictable, governed by probability — was one of the
first cracks in the deterministic worldview, and Curie
had spent her career measuring it.

She continued her research until her health failed.  The
radiation that made her career also killed her.  She was
interred in the Panthéon in 1995 — the first woman
honored there for her own achievements.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- /Recherches sur les substances radioactives/ (1903).
  Doctoral thesis documenting the discovery of polonium
  and radium.
- Nobel Prize in Physics (1903).  Shared with Pierre
  Curie and Henri Becquerel for research on radiation.
- Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1911).  For the discovery
  of radium and polonium and the isolation of pure
  radium.
- Mobile X-ray units (1914–1918).  Designed and operated
  radiological vehicles on the front lines during WWI.

**** After Solvay

- /Radioactivité/ (1935, posthumous).  Two-volume
  treatise summarizing her life's work on radioactivity.
- Curie Institute research program (1920s–1934).
  Trained the next generation of radioactivity
  researchers, including her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie
  who won her own Nobel in 1935.
