** Scope Note

German mathematician (1845–1918).  Created set theory
and proved the existence of multiple infinities — the
first demonstration that the mathematical universe was
stranger and wilder than anyone had imagined.  Opposed
by Kronecker, defended by Hilbert.  Suffered recurring
mental illness.

** Notes

Georg Ferdinand Ludwig Philipp Cantor (1845–1918).  Born
in Saint Petersburg to a German-Danish family, spent his
entire academic career at the University of Halle — a
provincial institution far from the major mathematical
centers.

His diagonal argument (1891) proved the real numbers are
uncountable — there are strictly more real numbers than
natural numbers, even though both sets are infinite.
This was the first proof that infinity comes in
different sizes, and it shattered the assumption that
infinity was a single, manageable concept.

Leopold Kronecker, one of the most powerful
mathematicians in Germany, attacked Cantor's work as
mathematical pathology and actively blocked his
advancement.  "God made the integers; all else is the
work of man," Kronecker declared.  Hilbert defended
Cantor, calling set theory "the finest product of
mathematical genius" and "a paradise from which no one
shall drive us."

Cantor suffered recurring bouts of severe depression,
was hospitalized multiple times, and spent his final
years in a sanatorium.  He died in 1918, impoverished,
during the last winter of the Great War.

Cantor's work was the first crack in the deterministic
dream of a single, complete mathematics.  If there were
multiple infinities — an infinite hierarchy of them, in
fact — then the mathematical universe was not the tidy,
fully surveyable edifice that nineteenth-century
mathematicians had assumed.  His continuum hypothesis —
that there is no infinity between the integers and the
reals — could be neither proved nor disproved within
standard set theory, as Paul Cohen showed in 1963.  The
foundations themselves were underdetermined.  This was
the first tremor; Gödel's incompleteness theorems
(1931) and the Bohr-Einstein debate at Solvay were
aftershocks of the same earthquake.

The opposition Cantor faced was not just intellectual
disagreement — it was visceral hostility.  Kronecker
used his institutional power to block Cantor's
appointments and publications.  The stress contributed
to Cantor's mental breakdowns, though modern historians
note that his depression likely had organic causes
independent of professional persecution.  What matters
for the Solvay narrative is the pattern: a radical idea
that overturns foundational assumptions, met by
establishment resistance that is partly intellectual
and partly social, with enormous personal cost to the
originator.

Hilbert's defense of Cantor was more than collegial
loyalty.  Hilbert saw set theory as the foundation on
which all of mathematics could be rebuilt — his program
of formalization depended on it.  When Gödel showed
that Hilbert's program was impossible, Cantor's
infinities survived while the dream of complete
formalization did not.  The paradise Hilbert promised
turned out to be wilder and stranger than even Cantor
had imagined.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- Proof of the uncountability of the reals (1874).
  First demonstration that some infinities are larger
  than others.
- Diagonal argument (1891).  Elegant proof technique
  showing that no enumeration of real numbers can be
  complete.
- Continuum hypothesis (1878).  Conjecture that there
  is no set whose cardinality lies strictly between
  the integers and the reals.

**** After Solvay

- Vindication by Hilbert (1926).  Hilbert's public
  defense of set theory as "a paradise from which no
  one shall drive us."
- Cohen's independence proof (1963, posthumous
  vindication).  Paul Cohen proved the continuum
  hypothesis is independent of standard set theory —
  it can be neither proved nor disproved.
