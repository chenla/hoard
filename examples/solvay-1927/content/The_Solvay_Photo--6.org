** Notes

Look at this photograph.

@@figure:Solvay_Conference_1927_Photograph--20@@

Twenty-nine people on the steps of the Institut
International de Physique Solvay, Brussels, October
1927. @@margin:Solvay_Conference_1927--11@@ Seventeen
of them are or will become Nobel laureates.  They have
gathered for the Fifth Solvay International Conference,
titled "Electrons and Photons."

But what was really at stake was the nature of reality
itself.

The most foundational assumption of the modern West —
that the universe is deterministic and fully knowable —
received its final dismantling in this
room. @@margin:Determinism--4@@ The people in this
photograph knew it was happening.  Some of them had swung
the hammer.

---

Werner Heisenberg had published the uncertainty principle
six months earlier. @@margin:Werner_Heisenberg--7@@ He
was twenty-five.  Niels Bohr had presented
complementarity at Como just weeks
before. @@margin:Niels_Bohr--7@@ Erwin Schrödinger's
wave equation was barely a year
old. @@margin:Erwin_Schrodinger--7@@ Louis de Broglie,
the 7th Duc de Broglie, had come to present a
deterministic alternative — pilot-wave theory — and
watched Wolfgang Pauli demolish it in the
room. @@margin:Louis_de_Broglie--7@@ Whether Pauli's
objection was actually fatal — David Bohm would show it
wasn't, twenty-five years later — is one of the great
what-ifs in physics. @@margin:David_Bohm--7@@ A viable
alternative to Copenhagen was killed that
week. @@margin:Copenhagen_Interpretation--4@@ The road
not taken.

Albert Einstein, the most famous scientist alive, spent
the conference constructing thought experiments to break
quantum mechanics. @@margin:Albert_Einstein--7@@  Bohr
spent the conference dismantling them, sometimes staying
up all night.  Paul Ehrenfest, watching from the back
row, wrote that it was "like a game of chess.  Einstein
all the time with new examples... Bohr from out of
philosophical smoke clouds constantly searching for the
tools to crush one example after the
other." @@margin:Paul_Ehrenfest--7@@

Bohr won.  Not by proving Einstein wrong — Einstein's
concerns about locality would prove prescient — but by
demonstrating that the Copenhagen interpretation was
internally consistent and experimentally productive.  The
physics community followed Bohr.  The old certainty was
gone.

---

What makes this photograph extraordinary is not just who
is in it, but /when/ they are in
it. @@margin:Solvay_Conference_1927_Photograph--20@@

Max Planck sits in the front row, sixty-nine years
old. @@margin:Max_Planck--7@@ He started all of this.
In December 1900, to explain black-body radiation, he
introduced the quantum of action — energy comes in
discrete packets.  He called it "an act of desperation."
He spent the next decade trying to reconcile his own
discovery with classical physics.  He failed.  Now he
sits and watches the young men take his quantum to places
he never intended.  Born's probabilistic interpretation,
Heisenberg's uncertainty, Bohr's complementarity — these
are not refinements of his idea.  They are a revolution
built on his foundation.  Like Einstein, he is
uncomfortable.  Unlike Einstein, he does not fight.  He
listens.

Marie Curie sits between Hendrik Lorentz and
Einstein. @@margin:Marie_Curie--7@@ She holds two Nobel
Prizes — Physics and Chemistry — and remains the only
person to have won in two different sciences.  Her
husband Pierre has been dead for twenty-one years.
Decades of handling radioactive materials without
protection have ravaged her health: her fingers are
burned, her eyesight is failing, she carries test tubes
of radium in her pockets.  She will not live another
seven years.  She looks tired, resolute, and entirely
unimpressed by the theoretical fireworks around her.

Lorentz, seventy-four, has chaired every Solvay
Conference since the first in
1911. @@margin:Hendrik_Lorentz--7@@ His transformations
became the mathematical foundation of Einstein's special
relativity.  He is perhaps the only person in the room
both Einstein and Bohr would defer to.  He will be dead
in four months.  When he dies in February 1928, Einstein
and Curie attend his funeral.

Paul Dirac, twenty-five, completed his PhD just a year
ago. @@margin:Paul_Dirac--7@@ His transformation theory
had already shown that Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and
Schrödinger's wave mechanics were the same theory in
different mathematical clothing.  He says almost nothing
at the conference.  Within a year he will publish the
Dirac equation, predict antimatter, and change physics
again.  In the photograph he looks slightly elsewhere, as
if the social occasion is a minor inconvenience
interrupting something more important happening inside
his head.

Wolfgang Pauli, twenty-seven, is already
feared. @@margin:Wolfgang_Pauli--7@@ His exclusion
principle is two years old.  He is the conference's
executioner.  "Not even wrong" is his dismissal for
theories too vague to be tested.  He stands next to
Heisenberg — friends and rivals since student days.  The
/Knabenphysik/ in full force: the physics of boys.

And then there is Paul Ehrenfest.  Forty-seven.  The
conference's gadfly, the man who asks the devastating
question.  He is close friends with both Einstein and
Bohr.  He stays at Einstein's house; he stays at Bohr's.
He cannot choose between them.

Six years from now, on 25 September 1933, Ehrenfest will
shoot his youngest son Wassik and then himself.  Wassik
had Down syndrome.  Ehrenfest was Jewish.  Hitler had
come to power eight months earlier.  Ehrenfest knew what
was coming for a disabled Jewish child in the Europe that
was taking shape — and he knew he would not be there to
protect him.

His suicide note is addressed to Bohr, Einstein, and the
other friends from this circle.  He writes that he can no
longer keep up with physics.  But the note does not fully
explain the act.  The physics was one thing.  His son was
another.

In the photograph he stands in the back row, looking
alert and engaged.  He has six years left.

---

I keep coming back to this photograph because of what it
contains: not just the people, but the /moment/.  The
last week when anyone could seriously argue that the
universe was a clockwork mechanism, fully knowable, fully
controllable.

We don't usually think of this as a world-historical
event.  The industrial revolution, the world wars, the
atomic bomb — these are the markers we use.  But the
shockwave that began in this room in October 1927 has
propagated further and deeper than any of them.

Within five years, Kurt Gödel proved that mathematics
itself could never be both complete and consistent —
there would always be true statements that could not be
proven. @@margin:Kurt_Godel--7@@ John von Neumann, who
had been working on the mathematical foundations of
quantum mechanics, recognized the implications
immediately. @@margin:John_von_Neumann--7@@ He proved
the complementary result: if a system of mathematics is
complete, it is necessarily inconsistent — and with an
inconsistent system, you can prove anything you like.
From here to eternity, mathematicians would have to
choose.

Von Neumann went on to build the first modern computer —
he called it the MANIAC — and along the way helped design
the hydrogen bomb, invented game theory, and laid the
theoretical foundations for self-reproducing machines and
artificial life. @@margin:The_MANIAC_Computer--4@@ On
his deathbed, he whispered that a thinking machine "would
have to grow, not be built... understand language... and
play, like a child."

In March 2016, nearly ninety years after the Solvay
photograph was taken, an artificial intelligence called
AlphaGo played the world's greatest Go player, Lee
Sedol, in Seoul. @@margin:AlphaGo_Move_37--11@@ In Game
2, AlphaGo played move 37 — a shoulder hit on the fifth
line that violated three thousand years of accumulated
human wisdom about Go.  AlphaGo's own system rated the
move as having a 1-in-10,000 probability.  Fan Hui,
watching from the commentary room, wrote: "It's not a
human move.  I've never seen a human play this move."

Move 37 was the moment the shockwave from Brussels
reached the general public.  Not as a physics result that
most people would never understand, but as a lived
experience: a machine had done something beautiful and
beyond human comprehension, and it had done it on its
own.

Benjamín Labatut's novel /The MANIAC/ traces this entire
arc — from Ehrenfest watching the Bohr-Einstein debates
to AlphaGo's move 37. @@margin:The_MANIAC_Novel--6@@
That book, and this photograph, are where this story
begins.

There is a reason I built a tool to understand this
photograph.

Most of what we know about these twenty-nine people comes
from sources that flatten them across time —
encyclopedias, biographies, Wikipedia articles.  Einstein
is "the physicist who developed general relativity."
Curie is "the first woman to win a Nobel Prize."  These
facts are true but they obscure something essential: who
these people were /that day/, in that room, at that
moment.

Heisenberg at twenty-five is not the same person as
Heisenberg at forty, staying in Germany under the Nazis,
running the uranium project.  Dirac at twenty-five,
silently thinking, is not Dirac at seventy, the reclusive
professor.  And Ehrenfest — alert and engaged in the back
row — is not yet the man who will murder his son.

Understanding people in temporal context — as they were
at a specific moment, not as the sum of everything they
ever did — is what historians do.  It's also what we
built [[https://github.com/chenla/hoard][Hoard]] to do.

The shockwave from Brussels is still propagating.  It
moved through physics, through mathematics, through
computation, through nuclear weapons, through artificial
intelligence.  At each stage, human understanding was
displaced a little further.  Ehrenfest could not follow
quantum mechanics.  Von Neumann's brain was destroyed by
cancer just as his computers surpassed human calculation.
Lee Sedol could not compete with AlphaGo.

This story — Screed — follows that shockwave.  It traces
the collapse of the deterministic paradigm that built the
modern world, and it asks what comes after.  Not as a
warning.  As a building plan.

Because the shockwave is arriving now — not as abstract
physics, but as the lived reality of artificial
intelligence, collapsing institutions, and a world built
on assumptions that turned out to be sand.  The
infrastructure we are building — Hoard is one piece of
it — is designed for the world that comes after
certainty.
