** Scope Note

American physicist who discovered the Compton effect — definitive evidence that electromagnetic radiation behaves as particles. Nobel Prize 1927. Later directed the Manhattan Project's Metallurgical Laboratory at Chicago.

** Notes

Arthur Compton (1892–1962).  American physicist.
Nobel Prize in Physics 1927 (shared with C.T.R.
Wilson) for discovering the Compton effect — the
increase in wavelength of X-rays when scattered by
electrons — which provided definitive evidence for
the particle nature of electromagnetic radiation.

The Compton effect was not merely another quantum
result — it was the experiment that settled the debate
over whether light truly behaves as a particle.  By
showing that X-ray photons transfer momentum to
electrons exactly as billiard balls would, Compton
provided evidence so clean that even skeptics of the
photon concept had to concede.

Professor at the University of Chicago, he later
played a key role in the Manhattan Project, directing
the Metallurgical Laboratory where the first
controlled nuclear chain reaction was achieved under
Enrico Fermi in December 1942.  At Solvay in 1927
he was only 35 — one of the younger attendees — but
his experimental result was already reshaping the
foundations of physics.
