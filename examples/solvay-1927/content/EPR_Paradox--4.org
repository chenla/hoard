** Scope Note

The Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen thought experiment (1935).
Argued that quantum mechanics must be incomplete because
it implied instantaneous correlations between distant
particles — "spooky action at a distance."  Einstein's
most sophisticated objection to Copenhagen.

** Notes

Published as "Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of
Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?" in /Physical
Review/ 47 (1935).  The paper defined "elements of
reality" and argued that if a physical quantity can be
predicted with certainty without disturbing the system,
it must correspond to something real — an element of
physical reality that quantum mechanics fails to
describe.

Bohr responded within months, rejecting the premise.
His reply argued that the EPR criterion of reality was
inapplicable to quantum systems — that the experimental
arrangement itself defines what can meaningfully be said
about the system.  The debate appeared purely
philosophical for nearly thirty years.

Then John Bell showed in 1964 that the question was
experimentally testable.  His theorem derived an
inequality that any local hidden-variable theory must
satisfy; quantum mechanics predicts violations.  Alain
Aspect's experiments (1982) confirmed quantum mechanics
and violated Bell's inequality — Einstein was wrong
about locality, but right that something profound was at
stake.  The EPR paper remains one of the most cited in
the history of physics.

The paper's structure deserves attention.  Einstein,
Podolsky, and Rosen did not simply assert that quantum
mechanics was wrong.  They defined a precise criterion
for what counts as an "element of reality": if, without
disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty the
value of a physical quantity, then there exists an
element of physical reality corresponding to that
quantity.  They then showed that quantum mechanics, for
entangled pairs, implied the existence of such elements
while simultaneously denying their reality.  The
argument was airtight /given its premises/ — and that
is exactly where Bohr attacked, rejecting the premise
that one could meaningfully speak of properties
independent of measurement context.

The debate appeared merely philosophical for thirty
years.  Physicists on both sides assumed the question
was untestable — a matter of interpretation, not
experiment.  This was wrong.  Bell's genius was to show
that local hidden-variable theories make different
/statistical predictions/ than quantum mechanics, and
that the difference is measurable.  Einstein died in
1955 without knowing that his most famous objection
would be experimentally resolved — and resolved against
him.  He never saw the answer.  The question he posed
in 1935 was not fully settled until Aspect's
experiments in 1982, and the definitive loophole-free
tests did not arrive until 2015.
