** Scope Note

The Japanese concept of the artisan-craftsman (職人)
whose mastery is inseparable from moral discipline —
the commitment to making each piece as well as it can
be made, regardless of whether anyone will notice.
Closely related to monozukuri (ものづくり), the art
of making things, where the process of production is
itself a form of knowledge.

** Notes

Shokunin is not merely "craftsman" in the Western
sense.  It carries an ethical weight closer to vocation:
the shokunin's obligation is to the work itself, not to
the customer's opinion of it.  Toshio Odate described
it as "the social obligation to work your best for the
general welfare of the people."

The ethic is visible in mundane production.  A sushi
chef spends years learning to prepare rice before
touching fish.  A blacksmith like Yukinori Shirataka
applied peerless forge technique to humble kitchen
knives rather than decorative swords.  The highest
expression of mastery is not the showpiece but the
everyday thing made perfectly — the Muji move.

This connects to the medieval cathedral masons who
carved the backs of capitals that would face the wall,
never to be seen.  "God sees" was the mason's answer.
The shokunin's version: "I would know."

Monozukuri extends the concept from the individual to
the production system.  Toyota's production system is
monozukuri scaled: respect for the process, continuous
improvement (kaizen), the belief that how you make
something matters as much as what you make.  This is
the bridge between craft and Dispersal — production
systems designed around mastery rather than extraction.
