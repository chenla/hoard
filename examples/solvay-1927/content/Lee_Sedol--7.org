** Scope Note

South Korean professional Go player (born 1983).
Considered one of the greatest Go players of the modern
era.  Lost 4-1 to DeepMind's AlphaGo in March 2016.
Retired from professional Go in 2019, citing AI as an
opponent that "cannot be defeated."

** Notes

Lee Sedol (born 1983 in South Korea).  9-dan
professional, 18 international titles.  Known for
creative, aggressive play and an ability to find moves
that other professionals had not considered.

His match against AlphaGo in March 2016 was watched by
over 200 million people.  He lost four games but won
Game 4 with his own "divine move" — move 78, a wedge
that exploited a blind spot in AlphaGo's evaluation.
DeepMind later confirmed that AlphaGo had assigned move
78 a near-zero probability.

He retired from professional Go in November 2019,
telling the press: "With the advent of AI in Go games,
I've realized that I'm not at the top even if I become
the number one through frantic efforts.  Even if I
become the number one, there is an entity that cannot
be defeated."

His retirement marked a quiet endpoint: the moment a
world-class human practitioner acknowledged that AI
had permanently surpassed human capability in his
domain.

Lee Sedol became a professional Go player at the age of
twelve — the youngest in South Korea at the time.  His
playing style was famously aggressive and unpredictable,
earning him the nickname "The Strong Stone."  He won
multiple world championship titles across different
tournament formats and was widely regarded as the
strongest player of the 2000s and early 2010s.  By the
time DeepMind challenged him, he was the obvious choice:
the human player whose creative, intuition-driven style
best represented what machines were supposed to be
unable to do.

Game 4 of the match — the one game Lee won — is as
significant as the four he lost.  His move 78, a wedge
played between two groups of white stones, was described
by commentators as a "God's touch" move.  AlphaGo had
evaluated it as having a 1-in-10,000 probability of
being played; when Lee played it, AlphaGo's subsequent
moves deteriorated rapidly.  The machine had a blind
spot, and Lee found it.  DeepMind's team later said
that Game 4 was more valuable to their research than
the four games AlphaGo won.

The match and its aftermath are a Solvay-scale event
for the twenty-first century.  Just as the 1927
photograph captured the moment determinism fractured in
physics, the Lee Sedol match captured the moment human
cognitive supremacy fractured in practice.  Lee's
retirement statement is the human side of that
fracture — not bitter, not dramatic, just a clear-eyed
acknowledgment that the world had changed.
