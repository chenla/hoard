** Scope Note

German-born theoretical physicist (1879–1955). Nobel Prize 1921 for the photoelectric effect. Architect of special and general relativity who helped launch quantum theory, then spent Solvay trying to prove it incomplete. The problems he raised — locality, realism, the EPR paradox — still haunt physics a century later.

** Notes

Albert Einstein (1879–1955).  German-born theoretical
physicist.  Nobel Prize in Physics 1921 (photoelectric
effect).  Special relativity (1905), general relativity
(1915).  Emigrated to the United States in 1933.  Spent
the last decades of his life at the Institute for Advanced
Study in Princeton, seeking a unified field theory.

One of the most recognized scientists in history.  His
work on the photoelectric effect helped launch quantum
theory, yet he spent much of his later career arguing
against its implications — particularly the abandonment
of locality and determinism.

Einstein is quantum theory's reluctant father.  His 1905
paper on the photoelectric effect — showing that light
comes in discrete packets of energy — was one of the
founding arguments for the quantum.  His 1924 work with
Satyendra Nath Bose on the statistical behavior of
photons (Bose-Einstein statistics) deepened the quantum
framework further.  Yet by the time of the 1927 Solvay
Conference he was already in open revolt against the
theory he had helped create.  The famous debates with
Bohr at Solvay were not the complaints of a man who
failed to understand quantum mechanics — they were the
objections of someone who understood it perfectly and
found its conclusions about reality unacceptable.

The 1935 EPR paper, written with Boris Podolsky and
Nathan Rosen, crystallized Einstein's objection: if
quantum mechanics is complete, then measuring one
particle can instantaneously affect a distant partner —
"spooky action at a distance."  Einstein believed this
proved quantum mechanics was incomplete, that hidden
variables must exist.  He was wrong about the hidden
variables, but the problems he identified were real.
Bell's theorem (1964) and subsequent experiments showed
that nature really is nonlocal in the way Einstein found
intolerable.  The EPR argument, intended to kill quantum
indeterminacy, instead became one of its most powerful
confirmations.

He spent his last two decades at Princeton pursuing a
unified field theory that would restore determinism at a
deeper level.  He never found it.  But the questions he
raised — about locality, realism, and what it means for
a physical theory to be complete — still define the
foundations of physics a century later.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- /On a Heuristic Viewpoint Concerning the Production
  and Transformation of Light/ (1905).  The
  photoelectric effect paper — light as discrete quanta,
  launching quantum theory.
- /On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies/ (1905).
  Special relativity — time and space are not absolute.
- /The Foundation of the General Theory of Relativity/
  (1915).  General relativity — gravity as spacetime
  curvature.
- /Quantum Theory of the Monatomic Ideal Gas/ (1924).
  Bose-Einstein statistics — quantum statistical
  mechanics for bosons.

**** After Solvay

- "Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical
  Reality Be Considered Complete?" (1935).  The EPR
  paper — the most precise statement of Einstein's
  objection to quantum indeterminacy.
- Unified field theory attempts (1930s–1955).  A
  decades-long search for a theory that would subsume
  both gravity and electromagnetism, restoring
  determinism at a deeper level.  Never completed.
