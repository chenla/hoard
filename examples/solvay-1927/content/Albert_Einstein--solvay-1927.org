** Notes

October 1927.  Einstein is 48.  He has held the Nobel
Prize for six years (awarded 1921, for the photoelectric
effect — not for relativity, which the committee
considered too controversial).  General relativity is
twelve years old and confirmed by Eddington's eclipse
observations.  He is the most famous scientist alive.

But Einstein has come to Brussels to fight.  Quantum
mechanics — the theory his own photoelectric work helped
launch — has grown into something he cannot accept.
Bohr and Heisenberg's Copenhagen interpretation says the
universe is fundamentally probabilistic: particles do not
have definite properties until measured.  Einstein finds
this intolerable.

His objection is not mere conservatism.  He believes
quantum mechanics violates locality — that it requires
instantaneous action at a distance, "spooky action at a
distance" as he will later call it.  At the conference he
constructs thought experiment after thought experiment
to reveal contradictions in the quantum framework.  Bohr
answers each one, sometimes staying up all night to
find the rebuttal.

Einstein will lose this debate — not because he is wrong
about locality (the EPR paradox he will publish in 1935
identifies a real and still-unresolved tension), but
because the Copenhagen interpretation works.  It
predicts experimental results with extraordinary
precision.  Physics will move on without him.

He sits in the front row of the photograph, arms crossed,
looking neither defeated nor persuaded.
