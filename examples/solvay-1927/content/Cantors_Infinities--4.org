** Scope Note

Georg Cantor's proof that infinity is not one thing but
many — the real numbers are uncountably infinite,
strictly larger than the natural numbers.  The diagonal
argument (1891) was the first crack in the deterministic
dream of a single, complete mathematics.

** Notes

Cantor showed that the power set of any set has strictly
greater cardinality than the set itself.  This means
there is no largest infinity — the hierarchy of
infinities extends without end.  His diagonal argument
(1891) proved the uncountability of the real numbers by
showing that any proposed enumeration must miss at least
one element.

His continuum hypothesis — whether there is an infinity
between the naturals and the reals — turned out to be
independent of the standard axioms of set theory.
Gödel showed in 1940 that the hypothesis cannot be
disproven from ZFC; Cohen showed in 1963 that it cannot
be proven either.  A mathematical question with no
answer — precisely the kind of undecidability that
Hilbert's program was designed to prevent.

Cantor was driven to despair by opposition from Leopold
Kronecker, who called his work mathematical pathology
and blocked his career advancement.  Hilbert defended
him, calling set theory "the finest product of
mathematical genius."  Cantor suffered recurring bouts
of depression and spent his final years in a sanatorium,
dying in 1918.

The independence of the continuum hypothesis — proved
by Cohen in 1963 using the method of forcing — is one
of the deepest results in the foundations of
mathematics.  It means that within the standard axioms
of set theory, the question "how many real numbers are
there?" has no definite answer.  The axioms are too
weak to decide.  This is not Godel's incompleteness
(which says some truths are unprovable) but something
stranger: the mathematical universe branches, and
different extensions of ZFC give different answers,
all equally consistent.  Undecidability is not a bug
in the system — it is the system.
