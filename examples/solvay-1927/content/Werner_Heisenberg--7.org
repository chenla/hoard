** Scope Note

German physicist (1901–1976). Nobel Prize 1932. Published the uncertainty principle at 25 — not a practical limitation but a fundamental feature of nature. Co-architect of the Copenhagen interpretation with Bohr and the leading figure of the Knabenphysik generation that rewrote the rules of matter.

** Notes

Werner Karl Heisenberg (1901–1976).  German theoretical
physicist.  Nobel Prize in Physics 1932 (creation of
quantum mechanics).  Formulated matrix mechanics (1925)
and the uncertainty principle (1927).

Led Germany's nuclear energy project during World War II
— the extent of his commitment to building a weapon
remains debated.  After the war, helped rebuild German
physics and directed the Max Planck Institute for Physics.

He was twenty-three years old when he invented matrix
mechanics in the summer of 1925, working alone on the
island of Helgoland while recovering from hay fever.
The paper, which dispensed with visualizable electron
orbits entirely and replaced them with abstract
mathematical tables of observable quantities, was so
radical that even his mentor Max Born needed weeks to
recognize that the "tables" were matrices.  Heisenberg
had reinvented matrix algebra from scratch without
knowing the mathematics already existed.

Two years later, at twenty-five, he published the
uncertainty principle: the position and momentum of a
particle cannot both be known with arbitrary precision.
This was not a statement about the limitations of
measurement instruments — it was a claim about the
structure of nature itself.  Together with Bohr's
complementarity, the uncertainty principle formed the
philosophical foundation of the Copenhagen
interpretation.  At the 1927 Solvay Conference,
Heisenberg was the youngest revolutionary in a room
full of them.

The wartime years cast a long shadow.  Heisenberg led
the German uranium project but never produced a weapon
— whether by incompetence, sabotage, or moral choice
remains one of the most debated questions in the history
of science.  The Farm Hall transcripts, secretly recorded
conversations among captured German physicists after
Hiroshima, reveal a man visibly shaken by the American
success and scrambling to explain why his own effort
had failed.  After the war he rebuilt German physics
institutions and became the first president of the
German Research Council, but his wartime choices followed
him for the rest of his life.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- "Über quantentheoretische Umdeutung kinematischer
  und mechanischer Beziehungen" (1925).  The matrix
  mechanics paper — the first complete formulation of
  quantum mechanics.
- "Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der
  quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik" (1927).
  The uncertainty principle — position and momentum
  cannot both be precisely determined.

**** After Solvay

- Nobel Prize in Physics (1932).  Awarded for "the
  creation of quantum mechanics."
- German uranium project (1939–1945).  Led the Nazi
  nuclear research effort; never produced a weapon.
- /Physics and Philosophy: The Revolution in Modern
  Science/ (1958).  Heisenberg's own philosophical
  account of the quantum revolution.
- Max Planck Institute for Physics (1946–1970).
  Directed the rebuilt German physics institute in
  Göttingen, then Munich.
