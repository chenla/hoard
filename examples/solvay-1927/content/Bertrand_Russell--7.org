** Scope Note

British philosopher, logician, and mathematician
(1872–1970).  Nobel Prize in Literature 1950.
Co-authored /Principia Mathematica/ with Whitehead — the
most ambitious attempt to ground mathematics in pure
logic.  Russell's paradox had exposed the cracks;
/Principia/ was the repair job; Gödel showed the cracks
were structural.

** Notes

Bertrand Arthur William Russell (1872–1970).  Third Earl
Russell.  Discovered Russell's paradox in 1901 — the set
of all sets that do not contain themselves — which
showed that naive set theory was inconsistent.  This was
not an obscure edge case; it struck at the foundations
of mathematics itself.

He spent the next decade with Alfred North Whitehead
building /Principia Mathematica/ to repair the damage.
The three volumes (1910–1913) attempted to derive all
mathematical truths from pure logic, constructing an
elaborate type hierarchy to avoid the paradoxes.  It
was the most ambitious formalization project ever
attempted — and it assumed that such formalization was
possible.  Gödel showed it was not.

Russell was also a major figure in analytic philosophy,
a prolific popular writer, and a political activist.
He was imprisoned for pacifism during WWI, helped found
the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, and remained
publicly active into his nineties.

The trajectory from logic to politics is not a detour —
it is the arc of a mind that watched the foundations
break.  Russell had staked his intellectual life on the
belief that mathematics, and by extension rational
thought itself, could be made rigorous and complete.
When Gödel proved in 1931 that any sufficiently powerful
formal system must contain truths it cannot prove,
Russell's program was finished.  He had already moved on
to epistemology and political philosophy, but the shift
was not just personal restlessness.  If the formal
foundations could not hold, then the work of making the
world rational had to be done by other means.

The Russell-Einstein Manifesto of 1955 was his most
consequential political act.  Co-signed with Einstein
(who died days after signing), it called for a
conference on the dangers of nuclear weapons and led
directly to the Pugwash Conferences on Science and
World Affairs.  Russell was by then in his eighties,
still writing, still protesting, still willing to be
arrested — he spent a week in Brixton prison at the
age of eighty-nine for a nuclear disarmament sit-in.

His /History of Western Philosophy/ (1945) became an
unlikely bestseller and funded the rest of his career.
He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950 "in
recognition of his varied and significant writings in
which he champions humanitarian ideals and freedom of
thought."  He died in 1970 at ninety-seven, having
lived long enough to see the formal dreams of his youth
replaced by the probabilistic, uncertain, incomplete
world he had spent the second half of his life trying
to navigate.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- Russell's paradox (1901).  Demonstrated that naive
  set theory is inconsistent — the set of all sets
  that do not contain themselves.
- /Principia Mathematica/ (1910–1913, with Whitehead).
  The most ambitious attempt to derive all mathematics
  from pure logic.
- "On Denoting" (1905).  Foundational paper in analytic
  philosophy on the theory of descriptions.

**** After Solvay

- /History of Western Philosophy/ (1945).  Popular
  survey that became a bestseller and funded his later
  career.
- Nobel Prize in Literature (1950).  For his body of
  varied and significant writings championing
  humanitarian ideals.
- Russell-Einstein Manifesto (1955).  Called for a
  conference on nuclear disarmament; led to the
  Pugwash Conferences.
