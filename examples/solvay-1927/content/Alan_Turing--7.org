** Scope Note

British mathematician (1912–1954).  Formalized the
concept of computation with the Turing machine (1936),
proving the halting problem undecidable — the
computational echo of Gödel's incompleteness.  Broke
the Enigma code at Bletchley Park.  Proposed the Turing
test for machine intelligence.  Prosecuted for
homosexuality, died of cyanide poisoning at 41.

** Notes

Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954).  His 1936 paper "On
Computable Numbers, with an Application to the
Entscheidungsproblem" defined the theoretical limits of
computation — answering Hilbert's decision problem in
the negative, independently of Alonzo Church.  The
Turing machine became the foundational model of
computation: a mathematical proof that some questions
are undecidable, mirroring Gödel's incompleteness in a
different formal register.

During the Second World War, his work at Bletchley Park
breaking German cipher systems — particularly the
Enigma machine — was a decisive factor in the Allied
victory.  The scale of the operation was enormous: at
its peak, Bletchley employed over 10,000 people, and
Turing's theoretical insights made the entire enterprise
possible.

After the war, he designed the ACE computer at the
National Physical Laboratory and worked on morphogenesis
at Manchester — using mathematical models to understand
how biological patterns emerge from chemical processes.
In 1952 he was convicted of gross indecency under
British law; he accepted chemical castration to avoid
prison.  He died on June 7, 1954, officially ruled a
suicide.  He received a royal pardon in 2013.

Turing is the computational echo of Gödel.  Where Gödel
showed that formal systems cannot prove all true
statements about arithmetic, Turing showed that no
algorithm can decide whether an arbitrary program will
halt — the halting problem.  The two results are
formally equivalent: they define the same boundary from
different directions.  Together they established that
no deterministic procedure can answer all well-posed
questions, even in principle.

His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence"
posed the question that still defines AI: can machines
think?  Rather than attempting a philosophical answer,
he proposed an operational test — the imitation game,
now called the Turing test.  His final major work,
the 1952 paper on morphogenesis, was equally
revolutionary: a mathematical theory of how chemical
reactions produce biological patterns like spots and
stripes.  He was using computation to understand
biology — the same direction von Neumann was heading.
Both died before they could finish the turn.

*** Key Works

**** Before 1927

(Turing was 14 years old in 1927.)

**** After 1927

- "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the
  Entscheidungsproblem" (1936).  The Turing machine —
  a formal model of computation that proved the halting
  problem undecidable, answering Hilbert's decision
  problem in the negative.
- Enigma / Bletchley Park (1939–1945).  Designed the
  Bombe and contributed to the breaking of German
  cipher systems — a decisive factor in the Allied
  war effort.
- ACE computer design (1945).  Detailed design for an
  electronic stored-program computer at the National
  Physical Laboratory.
- "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950).  The
  Turing test — the first rigorous proposal for
  evaluating machine intelligence.
- "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" (1952).
  Mathematical models of biological pattern formation
  — reaction-diffusion systems that explain how
  organisms develop stripes, spots, and other
  structures.
