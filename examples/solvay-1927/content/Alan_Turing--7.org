** Scope Note

British mathematician (1912–1954).  Formalized the
concept of computation with the Turing machine (1936),
proving the halting problem undecidable — the
computational echo of Gödel's incompleteness.  Broke
the Enigma code at Bletchley Park.  Proposed the Turing
test for machine intelligence.  Prosecuted for
homosexuality, died of cyanide poisoning at 41.

** Notes

Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954).  His 1936 paper "On
Computable Numbers, with an Application to the
Entscheidungsproblem" defined the theoretical limits of
computation — answering Hilbert's decision problem in
the negative, independently of Alonzo Church.  The
Turing machine became the foundational model of
computation: a mathematical proof that some questions
are undecidable, mirroring Gödel's incompleteness in a
different formal register.

During the Second World War, his work at Bletchley Park
breaking German cipher systems — particularly the
Enigma machine — was a decisive factor in the Allied
victory.  The scale of the operation was enormous: at
its peak, Bletchley employed over 10,000 people, and
Turing's theoretical insights made the entire enterprise
possible.

After the war, he designed the ACE computer at the
National Physical Laboratory and worked on morphogenesis
at Manchester — using mathematical models to understand
how biological patterns emerge from chemical processes.
In 1952 he was convicted of gross indecency under
British law; he accepted chemical castration to avoid
prison.  He died on June 7, 1954, officially ruled a
suicide.  He received a royal pardon in 2013.
