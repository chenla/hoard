** Scope Note

Northern Irish physicist (1928–1990).  His 1964 theorem
transformed the Bohr-Einstein debate from philosophy
into experimental physics.  Worked at CERN.  Died of a
cerebral hemorrhage at 62, just as his work was being
recognized as one of the most profound results in
physics.

** Notes

John Stewart Bell (1928–1990).  Grew up in working-class
Belfast, the first in his family to attend university.
Studied at Queen's University Belfast, then moved to
CERN in Geneva where he spent his career designing
particle accelerators — his "day job."

His theoretical work on quantum foundations was done
largely in his spare time, a fact that says as much
about the culture of physics as about Bell himself.
Questioning the foundations of quantum mechanics was
considered eccentric at best; the prevailing attitude
was "shut up and calculate."  Bell calculated — and
his calculations showed that the question mattered.

His 1964 paper "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox"
is one of the most cited papers in the history of
physics.  It proved that the EPR debate was not merely
philosophical — it made experimentally testable
predictions.  He died suddenly of a cerebral hemorrhage
on October 1, 1990, the year before the first
loophole-free Bell tests were attempted.  He was widely
tipped for the Nobel Prize.

Bell's theorem is deceptively simple in structure.  It
shows that if particles have definite properties before
measurement (as Einstein believed) and if no influence
can travel faster than light (locality), then the
correlations between measurements on entangled particles
must obey a mathematical inequality.  Quantum mechanics
predicts — and experiments confirm — that this
inequality is violated.  Therefore, either realism or
locality (or both) must be abandoned.  The Bohr-Einstein
debate, which had been treated as a philosophical
disagreement for thirty-seven years, was suddenly an
experimental question.

Bell was openly sympathetic to Bohm's deterministic
interpretation and critical of the Copenhagen
orthodoxy.  His paper "Against 'Measurement'" (1990),
written in the year of his death, argued that the
concept of "measurement" in quantum mechanics was
incoherent — the theory used a word from everyday
language as if it were a precisely defined technical
term, when in fact no one could say exactly where
measurement began and ended.  This was not a marginal
complaint; it struck at the conceptual foundations of
how quantum mechanics was taught and practiced.

The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Alain Aspect,
John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger for experiments that
confirmed Bell's predictions.  Bell had been dead for
thirty-two years.  The Nobel committee cannot award
prizes posthumously, but the experimenters' work was
meaningless without Bell's theorem — every citation of
the 2022 prize is implicitly a citation of Bell.  He
remains the most significant physicist of the twentieth
century never to receive the Nobel.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- Accelerator design at CERN (1960s).  Day job
  designing particle accelerators, providing the
  financial stability for his foundational work.
- "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox" (1964).
  Proved that the EPR debate had experimentally
  testable consequences — Bell's theorem.

**** After Solvay

- "Bertlmann's Socks and the Nature of Reality" (1981).
  Accessible paper explaining Bell's theorem through
  the analogy of a professor's mismatched socks.
- "Against 'Measurement'" (1990).  Critique of the
  incoherent use of "measurement" as a primitive
  concept in quantum mechanics.
- Nobel Prize in Physics 2022 (posthumous influence).
  Awarded to Aspect, Clauser, and Zeilinger for
  experimental work directly based on Bell's theorem.
