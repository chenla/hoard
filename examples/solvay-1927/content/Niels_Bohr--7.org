** Notes

Niels Henrik David Bohr (1885–1962).  Danish physicist.
Nobel Prize in Physics 1922 (atomic structure and
radiation).  Founder of the Copenhagen interpretation of
quantum mechanics.  Established the Institute of
Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen (1921), which became
the world center for quantum research.

Bohr's complementarity principle and his debates with
Einstein over the foundations of quantum mechanics defined
the philosophical landscape of 20th-century physics.

His 1913 atomic model — electrons orbiting the nucleus
in quantized energy levels — was the first successful
application of quantum ideas to atomic structure.  It
was crude and would be superseded within a decade, but
it established the principle that nature operates in
discrete jumps rather than smooth transitions.  By the
mid-1920s, his Copenhagen institute had become the
training ground for an entire generation of quantum
physicists: Heisenberg, Pauli, Dirac, Gamow, Landau,
and dozens of others passed through its doors.

The complementarity principle, which Bohr introduced at
the 1927 Como lecture just weeks before Solvay, was his
philosophical answer to the wave-particle duality: an
electron is neither wave nor particle, but both,
depending on how you observe it.  These are not
contradictions but complementary descriptions of a single
reality.  This idea — that the observer's choice of
measurement determines what aspect of nature is revealed
— became the philosophical core of the Copenhagen
interpretation and the central point of contention with
Einstein.

Bohr won the debate at Solvay, and he knew it.  The
physics community largely accepted his framework, and
for decades "shut up and calculate" became the working
philosophy of quantum mechanics.  During the Second
World War, Bohr escaped Nazi-occupied Denmark to Sweden
and then to the United States, where he contributed to
the Manhattan Project.  After the war, he became one of
the earliest advocates for international cooperation on
nuclear energy and the peaceful use of atomic power —
lobbying Churchill and Roosevelt directly, usually
without success.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- /On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules/ (1913).
  The Bohr model — quantized electron orbits, the first
  quantum theory of atomic structure.
- /The Correspondence Principle/ (1920).  Quantum
  predictions must converge with classical physics in
  the limit of large quantum numbers.
- /The Quantum Postulate and the Recent Development of
  Atomic Theory/ (1927).  The Como lecture introducing
  complementarity — delivered weeks before Solvay.

**** After Solvay

- /The Copenhagen Interpretation/ (1930s).  Not a single
  paper but an evolving framework — observer-dependent
  measurement, wavefunction collapse, no reality
  independent of observation.
- "Discussion with Einstein on Epistemological Problems
  in Atomic Physics" (1949).  Bohr's own definitive
  account of the Bohr-Einstein debates, published in
  the Schilpp volume.
- /Atomic Physics and Human Knowledge/ (1958).
  Philosophical essays extending complementarity beyond
  physics into biology and epistemology.
