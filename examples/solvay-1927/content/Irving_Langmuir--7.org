** Scope Note

American chemist and physicist. Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1932 for surface chemistry. Coined the term "plasma," developed the gas-filled lamp, and pioneered cloud seeding. One of the few industrial scientists to win a Nobel Prize — spent most of his career at General Electric.

** Notes

Irving Langmuir (1881–1957).  American chemist and
physicist.  Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1932 for
discoveries in surface chemistry — particularly the
Langmuir adsorption isotherm, which describes the
relationship between gas pressure and the amount
adsorbed on a surface.

Spent most of his career at the General Electric
Research Laboratory in Schenectady, New York — a
rare industrial scientist among the academic
physicists at Solvay.  At GE he developed the
gas-filled incandescent lamp, invented the Langmuir
probe for measuring plasma properties, and made
fundamental contributions to plasma physics (he
coined the term "plasma" by analogy with blood
plasma).

Also pioneered cloud seeding for weather
modification, a controversial line of research that
led to both military interest and public debate.
His presence at Solvay represented the industrial
research laboratory as a legitimate site of
fundamental science — a model that was still novel
in the 1920s.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- /Langmuir adsorption isotherm/ (1916–1918).
  Described gas adsorption on surfaces — the work
  that would win his Nobel Prize.

- /Gas-filled incandescent lamp/ (1913).  Practical
  invention that extended bulb life and efficiency.

**** After Solvay

- /Cloud seeding experiments/ (1946–1953).  Pioneered
  weather modification using silver iodide at GE.

- /Langmuir probe/ (1920s–1930s).  Diagnostic tool
  for measuring electron temperature and density in
  plasmas — still in use today.
