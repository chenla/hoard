** Notes

Max Born (1882–1970).  German-born British physicist.
Nobel Prize in Physics 1954 for the probabilistic
interpretation of the wave function — the Born rule,
which states that the square of the wave function's
amplitude gives the probability of finding a particle
at a given position.

At Gottingen he developed matrix mechanics together
with Heisenberg and Pascual Jordan, providing the
first rigorous mathematical formulation of quantum
mechanics.  Fled Germany in 1933 and settled in
Edinburgh.  His probabilistic interpretation was one
of the central ideas debated at the 1927 Solvay
Conference, where Einstein famously objected that
God does not play dice.

Born gave quantum mechanics its most radical claim: that
the wave function does not describe the electron itself
but the probability of finding the electron.  Before
Born's 1926 paper on collision theory, physicists could
hope that Schrödinger's wave equation described
something real and continuous — a vibrating charge
cloud, perhaps.  Born showed otherwise.  The wave
function's square gives probabilities and nothing more.
Nature, at the fundamental level, is irreducibly
statistical.  This was the specific idea Einstein
objected to — not the mathematics of quantum mechanics,
which he accepted, but Born's insistence that
probability was the final word rather than a placeholder
for deeper deterministic laws.

The Born-Jordan paper of 1925 was equally important: it
took Heisenberg's half-formed matrix mechanics and gave
it rigorous mathematical structure.  Born recognized
Heisenberg's "tables" as matrices and, with Jordan,
built the full algebraic framework.  Without Born's
mathematical formalization, Heisenberg's physical
insight might have remained a sketch.

Born was expelled from Göttingen in 1933 as a Jew, the
same purge that destroyed the greatest mathematics
department in the world.  He spent the rest of his
career at Edinburgh.  The Nobel Prize did not come until
1954, twenty-eight years after the Born rule — the
longest delay in the history of the Physics prize at
that time.  By then the rule was so foundational that
its late recognition was itself a statement about how
long it took the physics community to accept what
Born's interpretation truly meant.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- Born-Jordan paper (1925).  Rigorous mathematical
  formalization of Heisenberg's matrix mechanics —
  turned a physical insight into a complete theory.
- "Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge" (1926).  The
  Born rule — the square of the wave function gives
  the probability of finding a particle, founding the
  probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics.

**** After Solvay

- Born-Oppenheimer approximation (1927).  A method for
  separating electronic and nuclear motion in molecules
  — still fundamental to computational chemistry.
- Exile to Edinburgh (1933).  Fled Nazi Germany; spent
  the rest of his productive career in Scotland.
- Nobel Prize in Physics (1954).  Awarded for the
  probabilistic interpretation of the wave function —
  twenty-eight years after the original paper.
