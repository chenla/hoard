** Notes

Kurt Gödel (1906–1978).  Born in Brünn, Austria-Hungary
(now Brno, Czech Republic).  In 1931, at twenty-five, he
published the incompleteness theorems — arguably the most
important result in mathematical logic since Aristotle.

The first theorem shows that in any consistent formal
system capable of expressing basic arithmetic, there
exist statements that are true but unprovable within
the system.  The second shows that such a system cannot
prove its own consistency.

These results destroyed Hilbert's program — the attempt
to place all of mathematics on a complete, consistent,
decidable foundation.  Together with the Solvay
shockwave in physics, Gödel's work marked the end of
the deterministic dream in mathematics: the belief that
every question has a knowable answer.

Von Neumann grasped the implications immediately and
proved the complementary result.  Gödel emigrated to
the United States in 1940 and spent the rest of his
career at the Institute for Advanced Study, where he
and Einstein became close friends — two refugees walking
together through Princeton, united by their discomfort
with the probabilistic turn their fields had taken.

He died in 1978 of self-starvation, convinced his food
was being poisoned.

The arc of Gödel's career contains a remarkable
one-year reversal.  In 1929 he proved the completeness
theorem for first-order logic: every logically valid
statement in first-order predicate calculus is provable.
Mathematics, at the most basic level, works.  Then in
1930–1931 he proved the incompleteness theorems for
higher-order systems: any consistent formal system
powerful enough to express arithmetic contains true
statements that cannot be proven within it.  The move
from completeness to incompleteness — from "yes, it
works" to "but not all the way up" — happened in a
single year, and it destroyed the foundations of
Hilbert's program.

*** Key Works

**** After 1927

- /Die Vollständigkeit der Axiome des logischen
  Funktionenkalküls/ (1929).  The completeness theorem
  — every valid formula in first-order logic is
  provable.
- "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia
  Mathematica und verwandter Systeme" (1931).  The
  incompleteness theorems — any consistent system
  powerful enough for arithmetic contains true but
  unprovable statements, and cannot prove its own
  consistency.
- /Rotating Universes in General Relativity Theory/
  (1949).  A solution to Einstein's field equations
  in which time travel is theoretically possible —
  closed timelike curves in a rotating universe.
- /The Consistency of the Continuum Hypothesis/ (1940).
  Proved that the continuum hypothesis cannot be
  disproved from standard set theory axioms (the
  other direction, independence, was proved by
  Cohen in 1963).
