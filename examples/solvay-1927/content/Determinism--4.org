** Scope Note

The philosophical and scientific doctrine that every
event is necessitated by prior causes operating under
fixed laws.  The assumption that the universe is a
clockwork mechanism — fully knowable, fully predictable
— which undergirded Western science, governance, and
economics from Newton until its dismantling at the Fifth
Solvay Conference in 1927.

** Notes

Determinism is not one idea but a family of related
convictions: that the universe operates by discoverable
laws, that the future is in principle predictable from
the present, that causes produce effects in a chain that
extends backward to the beginning and forward without
limit.

Laplace gave it its most famous expression in 1814: an
intellect that knew the position and momentum of every
particle in the universe could, using Newton's laws,
calculate the entire future and reconstruct the entire
past.  This "Laplace's demon" was not considered
metaphorical.  It was the logical consequence of
Newtonian mechanics taken seriously.

The collapse began with Planck's quantum of action in
1900, accelerated through Einstein's photoelectric effect
and Bohr's atomic model, and reached its crisis at
Solvay in 1927.  Heisenberg's uncertainty principle
showed that position and momentum could not both be known
precisely — not as a practical limitation, but as a
fundamental feature of nature.  The universe was not a
clockwork.  It never had been.

The shockwave from this collapse propagated far beyond
physics: through Gödel's incompleteness (mathematics
cannot be both complete and consistent), through
computation (the halting problem), through biology
(evolution as undirected process), through economics
(markets as complex adaptive systems), and finally
through artificial intelligence (machines that learn
rather than execute).

The consequences extend far beyond physics.  Determinism
was not merely a scientific hypothesis — it was the
operating system for three centuries of Western
civilization.  Governance was built on it: the belief
that rational laws, rationally applied, would produce
predictable and optimal outcomes.  Economics was built
on it: general equilibrium theory assumed markets could
be modeled as closed systems with deterministic
solutions.  Industrial production was built on it:
Taylorism and scientific management treated workers and
processes as components of a knowable machine.  When
determinism collapsed in physics, these downstream
systems did not update.  They continued operating on
clockwork assumptions in a world that had been shown to
be probabilistic.  This is the core of the Screed arc:
the shockwave from 1927 has still not fully propagated
through the institutions that run the world.  We are
governed by Laplace's ghost.
