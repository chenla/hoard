** Scope Note

The secret American program (1942–1946) that built the
first nuclear weapons.  The first industrial application
of quantum physics — turning the abstract probabilities
debated at Solvay into the most destructive force in
history.  Von Neumann designed the implosion lens that
made the plutonium bomb possible.

** Notes

The Manhattan Project employed over 125,000 people
across multiple sites — Los Alamos, Oak Ridge, Hanford,
and dozens of others — at a cost of nearly $2 billion
in 1945 dollars.  It was directed by General Leslie
Groves and scientifically led by J. Robert Oppenheimer.

The physics that made it possible — nuclear fission,
quantum tunneling, neutron cross-sections — was the
direct offspring of the quantum revolution debated at
Solvay.  Many Solvay attendees were involved: Born's
former students, Bohr as a consultant, and von Neumann
as a key designer.  Von Neumann's contribution was
decisive: he designed the shaped-charge implosion lens
that compressed the plutonium core symmetrically,
making the Fat Man bomb feasible.

The Trinity test on July 16, 1945, and the bombings of
Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9)
demonstrated that quantum mechanics was not merely a
philosophical puzzle about the nature of reality — it
could reshape the physical world.  The abstract
probabilities that Bohr and Einstein debated over
breakfast had become the engineering basis for a weapon
that could destroy a city in seconds.
