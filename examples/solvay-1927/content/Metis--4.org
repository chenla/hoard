** Scope Note

Practical knowledge that can only be acquired through
experience in a particular place, time, and practice —
James C. Scott's term (borrowed from Greek) for the
local, embodied, tacit knowledge that high-modernist
schemes systematically destroy.  The knowledge a farmer
has of their particular field, a navigator of their
particular coast, a craftsman of their particular
material.

** Notes

Scott introduced metis as a central concept in /Seeing
Like a State/ (1998), drawing on the Greek word for the
cunning intelligence of Odysseus — practical wisdom
that cannot be reduced to rules or transmitted through
formal instruction.

The key argument: large-scale administrative schemes
fail catastrophically when they override metis.
Soviet collectivization destroyed the peasant farmers'
knowledge of local conditions.  Brasília's planned
geometry destroyed the informal knowledge embedded in
organic city growth.  Scientific forestry in Prussia
destroyed the forest's ecological complexity by
reducing it to timber yields.

In every case, the failure was not incompetence but
legibility — the state's need to simplify complex
local realities into readable, controllable abstractions.
The abstractions worked on paper.  They failed in the
field because they could not encode what the people on
the ground knew.

This is the deepest design constraint for Hoard.@@margin:Hoard@@
Metis must be preserved locally — subsidiarity@@margin:Subsidiarity@@
protects it.  But metis that remains purely local is
invisible to any larger coordination.  The challenge
is legibility without destruction: exposing enough
structure that a larger context can understand what a
local practice contains, without flattening it into
the kind of abstraction that kills the knowledge it
claims to represent.

Hoard's answer is the holon@@margin:Holons@@: a bounded scope that
preserves local organization while exposing its
contents through structured vocabulary.  The holon is
legible from outside without being controlled from
outside.
