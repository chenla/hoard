** Scope Note

Founded in 1912 by Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay. Organizes the Solvay Conferences on Physics and Chemistry — closed conclaves of 20–30 leading scientists, fully funded, with pre-circulated reports and structured discussion. Closer to a summit than a conference. The tradition continues to the present day.

** Notes

*** Origins

Founded in 1912 by Ernest Solvay, a Belgian chemical
industrialist whose soda ash process had made him one
of the wealthiest men in Europe.  Solvay had no formal
scientific training but a deep conviction that bringing
the best minds together in a room would accelerate
discovery.  He asked Hendrik Lorentz — universally
respected, multilingual, diplomatically gifted — to
organize the scientific program.

The first conference (1911) brought together Planck,
Einstein, Curie, Rutherford, Poincaré, and eighteen
others to discuss "The Theory of Radiation and Quanta."
It established the format that would persist for over a
century.

*** The Format

The Solvay Conferences are not conferences in the
modern sense.  They are closed conclaves — closer to a
summit or a concilium than to an academic meeting.

Roughly 20–30 scientists are invited by a scientific
committee.  There is no open registration, no audience,
no parallel sessions, no poster hall, no vendor
exhibition.  The invitees are the entire conference.
Everyone in the room is a participant.

Selected participants are commissioned to prepare
/rapports/ — formal reports on assigned topics.  These
are written up and circulated to all invitees before
the conference, so everyone arrives having already read
the material.  At the conference itself, the reports are
presented briefly.  The real substance is the structured
discussion that follows each presentation.

The discussions are transcribed and published alongside
the reports as conference proceedings.  For the 1927
conference, the proceedings were published in French and
remained poorly accessible to the English-speaking world
until Bacciagaluppi and Valentini's 2009 reconstruction.

Everything is funded by the Solvay Institutes — travel,
accommodation, honoraria.  The participants have no
financial barriers and no obligations beyond showing up,
preparing, and engaging.

*** Why This Format Matters

A modern scientific conference is designed for scale:
hundreds or thousands of attendees, 15-minute paper
slots, knowledge transfer that is largely
one-directional.  Most attendees never interact with the
speakers.  The format optimizes for breadth and
accessibility.

Solvay optimizes for depth.  Twenty to thirty people
who have already read each other's work, in one room,
arguing directly.  No audience to perform for.  No
time pressure that forces superficiality.  The format
assumes that the most important advances happen not in
presentations but in the collisions between prepared
minds.

The Bohr-Einstein debates at the Fifth Solvay
Conference could not have happened at a modern
conference.  They required a format where two people
could argue across multiple days — at breakfast, in
the discussion sessions, over dinner — with everyone
else in the room following every exchange.  Ehrenfest
could weep at the impasse precisely because he was
close enough to feel it.

The closest modern equivalents are Gordon Research
Conferences and Aspen Center for Physics workshops —
small, invitation-only gatherings with off-the-record
discussions.  But even these are larger and less
concentrated than Solvay.

*** Candor Without Anonymity

Chatham House Rules — formalized, coincidentally, in
1927 — create intellectual safety through anonymity:
speak freely, but nothing is attributed.  The cost is
that the discourse evaporates.  "A prominent physicist
raised objections to quantum indeterminacy" is useless
to history.

The Solvay format achieved something better.  It created
safety through peer parity in a closed room.  There was
no audience to perform for, no journalists, no junior
colleagues who might misquote you at a departmental
meeting.  You could say bold things — even wrong things
— because everyone else was operating at the same level
and doing the same.

Critically, the proceedings were published with full
attribution.  Einstein's objections are on the record.
Pauli's demolition of de Broglie is on the record.
Ehrenfest's anguish is on the record.  The disputes
became part of the scientific infrastructure — available
for future generations to learn from, build on, and in
Bohm's case, to vindicate a position that was dismissed
too hastily.

Attributed discourse is infrastructure.  Anonymous
discourse is safe but sterile.  The Solvay format found
the sweet spot: intimate enough for candor, documented
enough for posterity.

*** The Tradition

The Solvay Conferences continue to the present day,
roughly every three years.  The format has remained
largely unchanged.  The 2022 Solvay Conference on
Physics addressed "The Physics of Quantum Information."
The roster remains small, the discussions remain the
point, and the proceedings are still published.
