** Notes

October 1927.  Curie is 59.  She holds two Nobel Prizes
— Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911) — and remains the
only person to have won in two different sciences.  Pierre
has been dead for twenty-one years.

She is visibly exhausted.  Decades of handling radioactive
materials without protection have ravaged her health: her
fingers are burned, her eyesight is failing, she carries
test tubes of radium in her pockets and stores them in her
desk.  She will not live another seven years.

At the conference she represents the older generation of
experimentalists.  The theoretical battles between Bohr
and Einstein are not her war — her domain is the
laboratory, the measurable, the material.  But she
understands what is at stake.  She attended the first
Solvay Conference in 1911, the only woman then as now.
She has watched the theoretical edifice of classical
physics crack and fall across sixteen years of these
gatherings.

In the photograph she sits in the front row between
Lorentz and Einstein.  She looks tired, resolute, and
entirely unimpressed by the theoretical fireworks
around her.
