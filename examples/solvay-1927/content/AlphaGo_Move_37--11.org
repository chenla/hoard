** Notes

In March 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo played Lee Sedol, the
world's strongest Go player, in a five-game match in
Seoul.  AlphaGo won 4-1.

Move 37 of Game 2 became the defining moment.  AlphaGo
placed a stone on the fifth line in a position that no
human player would have considered.  Its own evaluation
rated the move as having a 1-in-10,000 probability of
being played by a human.  Fan Hui, the European Go
champion who had lost to AlphaGo months earlier and was
providing commentary, wrote: "It's not a human move.
I've never seen a human play this move."

The move won the game.  More importantly, it demonstrated
something that no previous AI achievement had: a machine
producing a creative, beautiful, winning decision that
exceeded human understanding.  Not by brute-force
calculation (as in chess) but by a learned aesthetic
that transcended its training data.

For the story of the deterministic shockwave, Move 37 is
the moment the abstraction became concrete.  Not a
physics result that most people would never understand,
but a lived experience broadcast to millions: a machine
had done something beautiful and beyond human
comprehension, on its own.

Benjamín Labatut's /The MANIAC/ uses Move 37 as the
endpoint of the arc that begins with Ehrenfest watching
the Bohr-Einstein debates at Solvay.

Lee Sedol's response to the move was visceral.  He stood
up, left the room, and took nearly fifteen minutes before
returning to the board.  Fan Hui, watching from the
commentary booth, first thought it was a mistake.  Then
he worked through the position and went quiet.  The move
was correct — better than correct.  When Sedol later
spoke about Move 37, he said it made him feel he was not
playing against a machine but against something he could
not name.

The parallel to Solvay 1927 is structural.  Both
moments mark the same threshold crossed at different
scales: a system producing valid results through a
process that its observers could not follow or verify
in real time.  At Solvay, the physicists could
reconstruct what the mathematics had done — eventually.
With Move 37, even professional players could not
explain why it worked until after the game was over.
The machine had moved beyond legibility.  That is the
shockwave, ninety years on.
