** Scope Note

The interpretation of quantum mechanics developed
primarily by Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg in the
mid-1920s.  Physical systems do not have definite
properties prior to measurement; the act of observation
collapses the wave function into a definite state.  The
interpretation that won at Solvay and dominated physics
for most of the twentieth century.

** Notes

The Copenhagen interpretation emerged from the
collaboration of Bohr and Heisenberg at Bohr's institute
in Copenhagen.  Its core claims: quantum systems exist in
superposition until measured; the wave function describes
probabilities, not physical reality; complementary
properties (position/momentum, wave/particle) cannot be
simultaneously determined.

At the Fifth Solvay Conference in 1927, the Copenhagen
interpretation faced its most serious challenge from
Einstein, who constructed thought experiments to reveal
contradictions.  Bohr answered each one.  The physics
community followed Bohr — not because determinism had
been disproven, but because Copenhagen worked: it
predicted experimental results with extraordinary
precision.

De Broglie's pilot-wave alternative was dismissed
(prematurely, as Bohm would later show).  For decades,
questioning Copenhagen was considered a sign of
confusion rather than insight.  John Bell's work in 1964
reopened the debate, and today multiple interpretations
compete — many-worlds, consistent histories, QBism — but
Copenhagen remains the default pedagogical framework.
