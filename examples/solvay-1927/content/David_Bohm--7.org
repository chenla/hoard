** Scope Note

American-Brazilian-British physicist (1917–1992).  Revived
de Broglie's pilot-wave theory in 1952, showing that a
deterministic alternative to Copenhagen quantum mechanics
was viable — twenty-five years after Pauli appeared to
have killed it at Solvay.

** Notes

David Bohm (1917–1992).  Born in Wilkes-Barre,
Pennsylvania.  Studied under Oppenheimer at Berkeley,
contributed to the Manhattan Project, then was hounded
out of the United States during McCarthyism for refusing
to testify against colleagues.

In 1952, while in exile in Brazil, he published a
deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics — now
called Bohmian mechanics or the de Broglie-Bohm theory.
He showed that Pauli's objection at Solvay, which had
killed de Broglie's pilot-wave theory, was not fatal.  A
consistent, deterministic, nonlocal hidden-variable
theory was possible.

The physics establishment largely ignored his work.
Copenhagen had won, and the question was considered
settled.  It would take another generation — John Bell's
theorem in 1964 — to reopen the foundational debate.

Bohm spent his later career at Birkbeck College, London,
developing his ideas on implicate order — the notion that
the visible, "explicate" world unfolds from a deeper,
enfolded reality.  His conversations with Krishnamurti
and his interest in consciousness made him an unusual
figure in theoretical physics: a rigorous mathematician
who insisted that physics had philosophical obligations
it was failing to meet.

His earlier work on plasma physics was itself significant.
At Berkeley under Oppenheimer, Bohm developed the theory
of Bohm diffusion — the anomalously fast scattering of
plasma across magnetic fields — which remained
unexplained by classical theory.  This work fed directly
into the Manhattan Project.  When HUAC demanded he
testify against Oppenheimer and others, he refused.
Princeton, where he had moved, refused to renew his
contract.  He lost his American passport and left for
Brazil, then Israel, then England — never returning to
the United States.

The de Broglie-Bohm theory was not just an intellectual
exercise.  It demonstrated that the "no hidden variables"
consensus that had hardened after Solvay was based on
unstated assumptions — specifically, that hidden-variable
theories must be local.  Bohm's theory was nonlocal, and
it reproduced every prediction of standard quantum
mechanics.  When John Bell set out to formalize what
exactly was wrong with hidden variables, it was Bohm's
work that showed him where to look.  Bell's theorem,
the most profound result in the foundations of physics,
is a direct descendant of Bohm's 1952 papers.

*** Key Works

**** Before Solvay

- Bohm diffusion (1949).  Theory of anomalous plasma
  diffusion across magnetic fields, developed during
  wartime research at Berkeley.
- /Quantum Theory/ (1951).  Textbook that gave the
  clearest exposition of the Copenhagen interpretation
  — and convinced Bohm himself that it was incomplete.

**** After Solvay

- "A Suggested Interpretation of the Quantum Theory in
  Terms of 'Hidden' Variables" (1952).  Two-part paper
  reviving de Broglie's pilot-wave theory and showing
  a deterministic QM was viable.
- /Wholeness and the Implicate Order/ (1980).
  Philosophical framework proposing that the visible
  world unfolds from a deeper, enfolded reality.
- /The Undivided Universe/ (1993, posthumous, with
  B.J. Hiley).  Mature formulation of Bohmian
  mechanics and its philosophical implications.
