** Scope Note

American-Brazilian-British physicist (1917–1992).  Revived
de Broglie's pilot-wave theory in 1952, showing that a
deterministic alternative to Copenhagen quantum mechanics
was viable — twenty-five years after Pauli appeared to
have killed it at Solvay.

** Notes

David Bohm (1917–1992).  Born in Wilkes-Barre,
Pennsylvania.  Studied under Oppenheimer at Berkeley,
contributed to the Manhattan Project, then was hounded
out of the United States during McCarthyism for refusing
to testify against colleagues.

In 1952, while in exile in Brazil, he published a
deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics — now
called Bohmian mechanics or the de Broglie-Bohm theory.
He showed that Pauli's objection at Solvay, which had
killed de Broglie's pilot-wave theory, was not fatal.  A
consistent, deterministic, nonlocal hidden-variable
theory was possible.

The physics establishment largely ignored his work.
Copenhagen had won, and the question was considered
settled.  It would take another generation — John Bell's
theorem in 1964 — to reopen the foundational debate.

Bohm spent his later career at Birkbeck College, London,
developing his ideas on implicate order — the notion that
the visible, "explicate" world unfolds from a deeper,
enfolded reality.  His conversations with Krishnamurti
and his interest in consciousness made him an unusual
figure in theoretical physics: a rigorous mathematician
who insisted that physics had philosophical obligations
it was failing to meet.
