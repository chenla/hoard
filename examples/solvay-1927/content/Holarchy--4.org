** Scope Note

Arthur Koestler's term for a hierarchy of holons —
entities that are simultaneously autonomous wholes and
dependent parts of larger wholes.  Every holon has a
dual tendency: self-assertion (maintaining its own
identity and boundaries) and integration (functioning
as a component of a larger system).  Neither tendency
can be eliminated without destroying the system.

** Notes

Koestler introduced the holon in /The Ghost in the
Machine/ (1967) to resolve the false dichotomy between
holism and reductionism.  A cell is a whole in its own
right, with its own metabolism, boundary, and internal
organization.  It is also a part of a tissue, which is
part of an organ, which is part of an organism.  At
every level, the entity is both whole and part — a
holon.

The holarchy is the structure formed by nested holons.
It is not a hierarchy in the command-and-control sense:
lower holons do not take orders from higher ones.
Instead, each level constrains and enables the levels
below and above it.  The organism constrains the organ
(you cannot have a liver without a body) but does not
micromanage it (the liver handles its own chemistry).

This maps directly onto Hoard's architecture.  A card
is a holon: a self-contained entity with its own
identity, scope note, and relations.  A holon
(wh:holon) is a higher-level holon: a bounded scope
containing cards with its own membership, ordering,
and expression preferences.  The Mother Hord is a
higher level still.  At each level, the entity manages
its own internal organization while exposing enough
structure for the next level to work with.

The key property is that holarchies resolve the
rhizome-vs-hierarchy tension.  Pure hierarchies are
brittle (all control is centralized).  Pure rhizomes
are illegible (no structure is visible).  Holarchies
provide bounded complexity: rooms for the rats, with
doors that connect to the corridor.

Wilber extended Koestler's holarchy into an integral
framework spanning matter, life, mind, and spirit.
Whether or not one follows Wilber's metaphysics, his
structural insight holds: holarchies are the only
organizational form that preserves both autonomy and
coordination across scales.
