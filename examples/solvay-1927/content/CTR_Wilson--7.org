** Notes

C.T.R. Wilson (1869–1959).  Scottish physicist.  Nobel
Prize in Physics 1927 (shared with Arthur Compton).
Inventor of the cloud chamber, a device that made the
tracks of charged particles visible for the first time.

Originally inspired by atmospheric phenomena observed
while working at the Ben Nevis meteorological
observatory in the 1890s, Wilson spent years
perfecting the expansion cloud chamber at the
Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge.  What began as an
attempt to recreate cloud formation in the laboratory
became one of the most important instruments in
physics.

The cloud chamber made the subatomic world visible
for the first time — charged particles left trails
of condensation droplets that could be photographed
and analyzed.  It became the essential tool of
particle physics for decades, enabling Carl
Anderson's discovery of the positron (1932) and
later the identification of the muon.  The device
dominated experimental particle physics until the
bubble chamber replaced it in the 1950s.
