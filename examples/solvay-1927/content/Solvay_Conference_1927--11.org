** Scope Note

The Fifth Solvay International Conference on Physics, held 24–29 October 1927 in Brussels. Twenty-nine physicists — seventeen of them Nobel laureates — gathered to debate "Electrons and Photons." The Bohr-Einstein debates that erupted here defined the philosophical fault lines of twentieth-century physics and marked the moment determinism lost its hold on science.

** Notes

*** The Conference

#+CAPTION: The Institut International de Physique Solvay, Parc Léopold, Brussels — the building on whose steps the famous group photograph was taken.
[[file:lib/media/institut-solvay--parc-leopold.jpg]]

The Fifth Solvay International Conference on Physics was
held 24–29 October 1927 at the Institut International de
Physique Solvay in Brussels.  The theme was "Electrons
and Photons" — the two entities whose behavior was
forcing physics to abandon its classical foundations.

This was not a conference in the modern sense.  The
Solvay Conferences were closed conclaves — twenty to
thirty scientists, personally invited, with no audience
and no outside attendees.  Reports were commissioned
from selected participants, circulated in advance, and
then discussed by the full group.  The format was
designed for direct argument between prepared minds,
not for presentations to a passive audience.  Everything
— travel, accommodation, honoraria — was funded by the
Solvay Institutes.

Twenty-nine physicists attended, seventeen of whom were
or became Nobel laureates.  Hendrik Lorentz, who had
presided over every Solvay Conference since the first in
1911, chaired the meeting.  He would die five months
later; this was his last conference.

The scientific secretaries were Auguste Piccard and
Émile Henriot.  Jules-Émile Verschaffelt handled
organizational duties.  Edouard Herzen served as
liaison to the Solvay family.

*** Reports

Six physicists were commissioned to prepare formal
reports, each addressing a facet of the quantum crisis.
These had been circulated before the conference; the
presentations were summaries, and the substance was in
the discussion that followed:

- *William Lawrence Bragg* — X-ray diffraction and the
  evidence for wave-like behavior of radiation
- *Arthur Compton* — The Compton effect and the particle
  nature of X-rays (direct conflict with wave theory)
- *Louis de Broglie* — Pilot-wave theory: a
  deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics in
  which particles are guided by a wave field
- *Max Born and Werner Heisenberg* — Matrix mechanics
  and the probabilistic interpretation of the wave
  function
- *Erwin Schrödinger* — Wave mechanics as an
  alternative mathematical framework
- *Niels Bohr* — Complementarity: the principle that
  wave and particle descriptions are mutually exclusive
  but both necessary

*** The Bohr-Einstein Debates

The formal reports set the stage, but the conference is
remembered for what happened in the discussions — and
this is where the Solvay format proved decisive.  At a
modern conference, Einstein and Bohr might have exchanged
questions after a 15-minute talk and never spoken again.
At Solvay, they were in the same room for six days,
eating together, arguing across sessions, with every
other participant following every exchange.

Einstein did not present a report.  Instead, he attacked
from the floor — devising thought experiments intended
to show that quantum mechanics was incomplete, that it
must be possible in principle to determine both position
and momentum precisely.

Each morning Einstein would present a new thought
experiment at breakfast.  Each evening Bohr would
produce a refutation.  The pattern repeated for days.
Ehrenfest, who was close friends with both men, moved
between them — and reportedly wept at the spectacle of
two great minds unable to reach agreement.

The debates did not resolve anything.  Bohr's
Copenhagen interpretation carried the working consensus,
but Einstein never accepted it.  He spent the rest of
his life devising increasingly sophisticated objections,
culminating in the EPR paradox of 1935.  The questions
he raised — about locality, realism, and the
completeness of quantum mechanics — were not answered
in his lifetime.  Some remain open today.

*** De Broglie's Pilot Wave

Louis de Broglie presented his pilot-wave theory as a
deterministic alternative to the emerging Copenhagen
consensus.  In this model, particles have definite
positions at all times, guided by a wave field — no
probability, no superposition, no collapse.

Wolfgang Pauli demolished it during the discussion
session, identifying what he argued were fatal
inconsistencies in the multi-particle case.  De Broglie
abandoned the theory and did not return to it for
twenty-five years.

In 1952, David Bohm independently reconstructed and
extended the pilot-wave approach, resolving Pauli's
objection.  The de Broglie-Bohm interpretation remains
the most developed deterministic alternative to
Copenhagen — proof that the question Einstein and
de Broglie raised at Solvay was never truly settled.

*** The Photograph

The group photograph taken on the steps of the Institut
International de Physique Solvay has become one of the
most famous images in the history of science.  Benjamin
Couprie, who documented several Solvay Conferences,
took the photograph.

It is an accidental portrait of a turning point.
Almost everyone in the frame contributed directly to the
destruction of the deterministic worldview that had
governed science since Newton.  Several would go on to
build the technologies — nuclear weapons, computers,
semiconductors — that emerged from that destruction.

*** Historical Context

The conference took place in a Europe that was fragile
and increasingly dangerous.  Belgium itself had been
devastated by the Great War less than a decade earlier.
The Weimar Republic was showing cracks.  Antisemitism
was rising across the continent — within six years,
Einstein, Born, Schrödinger, and others would be forced
into exile.

The physicists in Brussels in October 1927 were not
thinking about politics.  They were thinking about
electrons and photons.  But the world they were building
— a world without certainty, without determinism,
without the comforting fiction that everything could be
known and predicted — would collide with that political
reality in ways none of them could foresee.

*** Aftermath

The Fifth Solvay Conference did not settle the
interpretation of quantum mechanics.  It established the
battle lines.  Copenhagen became the working orthodoxy
— "shut up and calculate," as the later caricature had
it.  But the questions Einstein raised never went away.

The next Solvay Conference, in 1930, saw Einstein
return with a more elaborate thought experiment.  Bohr
defeated it using Einstein's own general relativity
against him.  The 1933 conference was the last before
the war scattered the participants across the globe.

What happened at Brussels in October 1927 propagated
outward like a shockwave — through Gödel's
incompleteness theorems (1931), through Turing's
halting problem (1936), through the Manhattan Project,
through the invention of the transistor and the
computer, through Bell's theorem (1964) and the
experimental confirmation of quantum nonlocality, and
finally through the emergence of machine learning and
artificial intelligence — machines that learn from
probability rather than execute from certainty.

The photograph captured the moment.  The shockwave is
still propagating.
