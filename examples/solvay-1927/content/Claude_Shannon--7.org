** Scope Note

American mathematician and electrical engineer
(1916–2001).  Founded information theory with his 1948
paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication."  Showed
that information could be quantified in bits and
transmitted reliably through noisy channels.  Made the
probabilistic nature of communication mathematically
precise.

** Notes

Claude Elwood Shannon (1916–2001).  His 1937 master's
thesis at MIT showed that Boolean algebra could be
applied to electrical circuits — demonstrating that any
logical statement could be implemented in hardware.
This was the theoretical foundation for digital circuit
design, and it has been called the most important
master's thesis of the twentieth century.

His 1948 paper at Bell Labs, "A Mathematical Theory of
Communication," created information theory.  It defined
entropy as a measure of uncertainty in a message,
established the concept of channel capacity, and
introduced the bit as the fundamental unit of
information.  The framework made the probabilistic
nature of communication mathematically precise —
information is defined in terms of what you don't know,
not what you do.

Shannon was also a legendary tinkerer.  He built
chess-playing machines, juggling robots, a
flame-throwing trumpet, and a device called the
"Ultimate Machine" whose sole function was to turn
itself off.  He spent his later years at MIT, where
he could often be found unicycling through the halls.
He was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease and died in
2001.

Shannon completed the triad that redefined the
deterministic landscape.  Where Gödel showed the limits
of formal proof and Turing showed the limits of
computation, Shannon showed that communication itself
is fundamentally probabilistic.  Information, as he
defined it, is measured by surprise — the less probable
a message, the more information it carries.  His
entropy formula is identical in structure to the
thermodynamic entropy of Boltzmann and the quantum
entropy of von Neumann.  The convergence is not
coincidence: all three describe the same underlying
relationship between certainty and ignorance.

His channel capacity theorem proved that reliable
communication is possible even through noisy channels,
up to a mathematically precise limit.  This was the
theoretical foundation for everything from modems to
deep-space probes to the internet.  The theorem's
proof was non-constructive — it showed the limit
existed without showing how to achieve it — and it
took decades of coding theory to approach Shannon's
bound in practice.  Modern 5G and satellite
communications operate within a fraction of a decibel
of the limit he calculated in 1948.

What makes Shannon's work essential to the Solvay
narrative is the completion of the arc: the 1927
conference established that nature is probabilistic at
the quantum level; Shannon showed that information —
the stuff of thought, measurement, and communication —
is probabilistic by definition.  The bit, his
fundamental unit, is a unit of uncertainty resolved.

*** Key Works

**** Before 1927

(Shannon was 10 years old in 1927.)

**** After 1927

- "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits"
  (1937).  Master's thesis — proved that Boolean
  algebra could be implemented in electrical circuits,
  the theoretical foundation for digital hardware
  design.
- "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948).
  Founded information theory — defined entropy as a
  measure of uncertainty, introduced the bit, proved
  the channel capacity theorem.
- "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems" (1949).
  Applied information theory to cryptography —
  proved the one-time pad is unbreakable and
  established the theoretical foundations of modern
  cryptanalysis.
