** Scope Note

American mathematician and electrical engineer
(1916–2001).  Founded information theory with his 1948
paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication."  Showed
that information could be quantified in bits and
transmitted reliably through noisy channels.  Made the
probabilistic nature of communication mathematically
precise.

** Notes

Claude Elwood Shannon (1916–2001).  His 1937 master's
thesis at MIT showed that Boolean algebra could be
applied to electrical circuits — demonstrating that any
logical statement could be implemented in hardware.
This was the theoretical foundation for digital circuit
design, and it has been called the most important
master's thesis of the twentieth century.

His 1948 paper at Bell Labs, "A Mathematical Theory of
Communication," created information theory.  It defined
entropy as a measure of uncertainty in a message,
established the concept of channel capacity, and
introduced the bit as the fundamental unit of
information.  The framework made the probabilistic
nature of communication mathematically precise —
information is defined in terms of what you don't know,
not what you do.

Shannon was also a legendary tinkerer.  He built
chess-playing machines, juggling robots, a
flame-throwing trumpet, and a device called the
"Ultimate Machine" whose sole function was to turn
itself off.  He spent his later years at MIT, where
he could often be found unicycling through the halls.
He was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease and died in
2001.
