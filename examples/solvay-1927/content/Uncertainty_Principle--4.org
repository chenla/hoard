** Scope Note

Heisenberg's 1927 principle.  Certain pairs of physical
properties (position/momentum, energy/time) cannot both
be known to arbitrary precision — not due to measurement
limits but as a fundamental feature of nature.

** Notes

Published by Heisenberg in March 1927 while Bohr was
away skiing.  The principle derives from the matrix
mechanics formalism Heisenberg had developed in 1925:
the non-commutativity of position and momentum operators
means their simultaneous precise determination is
mathematically impossible, not merely practically
difficult.

At the Fifth Solvay Conference in October 1927, the
uncertainty principle became the centerpiece of the
Copenhagen position.  Bohr folded it into his broader
complementarity framework, though Heisenberg and Bohr
disagreed about its interpretation — Heisenberg
emphasized disturbance by measurement, Bohr emphasized
the wave-particle duality itself.

Einstein spent the rest of his life constructing thought
experiments to circumvent it.  Each morning at Solvay he
would present a new scenario at breakfast; each evening
Bohr would produce a refutation.  The principle held.
It remains one of the most experimentally confirmed
results in all of physics.

The critical point, often misunderstood, is that the
uncertainty principle is not a statement about the
clumsiness of measurement instruments.  It is not that
we /disturb/ the particle by measuring it (though
Heisenberg himself sometimes described it that way,
which Bohr corrected).  It is that position and momentum
do not simultaneously /exist/ as precise values.  The
universe at its finest scale is not merely unknown but
unknowable — not because we lack better tools, but
because there is nothing definite there to know until a
measurement creates it.

The structural parallel to Gödel's incompleteness
theorems (1931) is not coincidence — it is the same
limit encountered in a different domain.  Heisenberg
showed that physical systems cannot be fully specified.
Gödel showed that formal systems cannot be fully
complete.  Turing showed that computation cannot be
fully decidable.  Three independent proofs, in three
different fields, all arriving at the same conclusion
within a single decade: that no system, physical or
formal, can contain a complete description of itself.
The deterministic dream — that everything is in
principle knowable — died three separate deaths.
