** Scope Note

The principle that decisions should be made at the
lowest level competent to handle them.  Not
decentralization for its own sake, but a constraint on
where authority lives: as close to the context as
possible, escalating upward only when local capacity is
genuinely insufficient.

** Notes

Subsidiarity originates in Catholic social teaching
(Pius XI, /Quadragesimo Anno/, 1931) but the underlying
pattern is far older.  It appears in federal governance,
in guild structures, in the relationship between a
monastery and its surrounding community.

The principle is biological before it is political.
Cells handle local chemistry; organs coordinate systems;
the organism responds to the environment.  No cell
consults the brain about ion exchange.  The brain does
not manage digestion.  Each level handles what it can,
and the system's resilience comes from this
distribution of competence.

In Hoard, subsidiarity operates through holons: each
holon is a bounded scope with its own membership,
ordering, and expression preferences.  A holon does not
need permission from a parent to organize its own
cards.  But it exposes enough structure — through
vocabulary and quads — that a larger context can
understand what it contains without needing to
micromanage it.

This is the Goldilocks zone for context: metis is
preserved locally, intent is exposed upward.  Too much
centralization destroys local knowledge.  Too much
autonomy destroys legibility.  Subsidiarity is the
constraint that keeps both alive.
