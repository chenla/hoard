** Notes

/The MANIAC/ by Benjamín Labatut.  Published 2023.
Structured in three parts: Paul Ehrenfest's suicide, John
von Neumann's life and the creation of the computer, and
the AlphaGo-Lee Sedol match.

Part one opens with Ehrenfest — the gadfly of the Solvay
conferences, close friend of both Einstein and Bohr — who
could not choose between their visions and could not keep
up with the physics.  His murder-suicide in 1933
(shooting his son Wassik, who had Down syndrome, then
himself) frames the human cost of the collapse of the
deterministic worldview.

Part two follows von Neumann from Budapest prodigy to
Princeton polymath: quantum mechanics, game theory, the
Manhattan Project, the stored-program computer.  The
MANIAC — Mathematical Analyzer, Numerical Integrator, and
Automatic Computer — was the machine he built at the
Institute for Advanced Study.

Part three is the AlphaGo match, told through Lee Sedol,
Fan Hui, and the DeepMind team.  Move 37 becomes the
endpoint: a machine producing something beautiful and
beyond human comprehension.

Labatut works in the boundary between fiction and history,
a form he also used in /When We Cease to Understand the
World/ (2020).  His method — historical research rendered
as intimate, sometimes hallucinatory narrative — makes
these ideas accessible without simplifying them.

The three-part structure is the argument.  Ehrenfest
is the human cost of the collapse — a man who could
not survive the loss of the world he understood.  Von
Neumann is the mechanism of the next world — the man
who built the machine that would eventually exceed its
builder.  Lee Sedol is the arrival point — the world's
best human player, defeated by something that learned
to play without understanding what it was doing.  The
arc runs from 1927 to 2016, from a hotel ballroom in
Brussels to a match hall in Seoul, and the shockwave
is continuous.

What Labatut achieves by blurring fiction and history
is access to interiority — to what it felt like to be
inside these moments, not merely what happened.  He
invents dialogue and inner states while remaining
faithful to documented fact.  The reader cannot always
tell which is which, and that is the point: the
historical record cannot tell us either.  The people
who lived through these ruptures did not have captions.
They had only the experience of the world shifting
under their feet.  Labatut recreates that texture.

In this way /The MANIAC/ does for the deterministic
shockwave what Screed is trying to do through a
different form: make the arc felt as a single lived
thing rather than a sequence of separate intellectual
events.  The book is a proof of concept for the
narrative strategy.
