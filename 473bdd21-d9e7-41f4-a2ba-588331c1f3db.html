Notes

October 1927. Pauli is 27. His exclusion principle is two years old. He is already feared.

His godfather was Ernst Mach — the philosopher- physicist whose name is on the speed of sound. He studied under Sommerfeld, who set him the task of writing a review of general relativity as a graduate student. The resulting monograph, published when Pauli was 21, was so good that Einstein praised it.

At this conference, Pauli is the executioner. When de Broglie presents his pilot-wave theory — a deterministic alternative to Copenhagen — Pauli dismantles it with a specific technical objection that de Broglie cannot answer. The theory dies in the room. De Broglie abandons it for twenty-five years. Whether Pauli's objection was actually fatal (Bohm will show in 1952 that it wasn't) or merely persuasive is one of the great what-ifs in the history of physics.

Pauli's cruelty is legendary. "Not even wrong" is his dismissal for theories too vague to be tested. He reduces grown physicists to silence. But his judgment is almost always right, which makes it worse.

He stands in the back row of the photograph, next to Heisenberg — friends and rivals since their student days in Munich. The Knabenphysik in full force: two men in their mid-twenties who have already changed physics and are not finished.
